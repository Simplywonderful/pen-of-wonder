In research, being able to communicate your findings matters as much as the findings themselves. A good presentation engages your audience, gets your results across and stays with people after you have finished. Yet many researchers struggle to turn months of detailed work into a short, compelling talk.

This guide covers how to structure a research presentation, the mistakes that most often lose an audience, and the habits that make a talk work. The example talk running through it is about a fictional study of brain plasticity after stroke; the study and its findings are invented for illustration.

## What is a research presentation?

A research presentation is a structured summary of your research: your objectives, methods, findings and conclusions, delivered to an audience. A paper can be long and detailed. A presentation has to be brief, clear and visual. Your job is to get the essence of your study across, accurately and persuasively, in the time you have.

## How to structure a research presentation

### Open with something that draws people in

The first minute sets the tone. Start with a short story, a striking fact or a question related to your research, rather than “Today I’m going to talk about…”. Check any fact you open with, and don’t promise more than your findings deliver.

**Example:** “After a stroke, some people regain abilities doctors thought were lost. Why do some recover so much more than others? That is the question our study set out to explore.”

### Say what you set out to do

State your aim, and what the audience will take away. It gives them a map, and helps them follow the rest.

**Example:** “In the next 15 minutes, I’ll explain what brain plasticity is, what we measured in our patients, and what our results suggest for rehabilitation.”

### Keep the methods simple

Describe your methods clearly, without jargon, and only in as much detail as the audience needs to trust your results. The full detail can go in a handout or a backup slide for questions.

**Example:** “We scanned the brains of 40 stroke patients before and after 12 weeks of cognitive therapy, using fMRI, which shows which parts of the brain are active during a task.”

### Show your findings visually

Use graphs, charts and images to show your results. A good visual makes complex data far easier to grasp and remember.

**Example:** a simple bar chart comparing improvement under two therapy methods, with the difference clearly labelled.

### Explain what it means

Say why your results matter, how they connect to wider questions, and what should happen next. Keep the claims in proportion to your evidence.

**Example:** “Our results suggest that targeted cognitive therapy may help some patients make more use of the brain’s capacity to adapt. A larger trial is needed to confirm this.”

### Finish clearly

Summarise your key points and leave the audience with one clear takeaway, or a question worth thinking about.

**Example:** “If the brain can reorganise itself after injury, which other conditions might respond to the same approach?”

A common structure for the whole talk is: introduction, background, methods, results, discussion and conclusion. Adjust it to fit your time and your audience.

## Presentation mistakes to avoid

### Overloading your slides

Cluttering slides with text is one of the most common mistakes. It overwhelms your audience and buries your key points. People can’t read a paragraph and listen to you at the same time.

**Instead:** put a few key words or one clear visual on each slide, and say the rest aloud.

### Reading to your audience

A talk read from slides or notes sounds flat, however good the research is.

**Instead:** rehearse until you can speak to the audience, not to the screen. Vary your tone, emphasise the key points and make eye contact. Tell the story of the research: “When we analysed the data, one result surprised us…”

### Poor visuals

Crowded charts, blurry images and inconsistent formatting distract from your message, and can mislead.

**Instead:** use clear, high-quality visuals that relate directly to what you are saying. Simplify charts to show the data that matters for your point.

### Forgetting the audience is there

A talk with no interaction can feel like a one-way lecture.

**Instead:** invite questions, ask for views, or use a quick poll where the setting allows. “Before I go on, how do you think this might apply in your own field?”

### Running over

Poor timing leads to rushed endings and unfinished talks, and running over is one of the quickest ways to lose goodwill.

**Instead:** rehearse several times against the clock, and plan a time for each section. For a 20-minute talk, you might allow 5 minutes for the introduction and background, 10 for methods and results, and 5 for the discussion and conclusion.

## Habits that make a talk work

### Break complex ideas into steps

Present one idea at a time, and use analogies to make abstract ideas concrete.

**Example:** “Think of the brain as a city’s road network. When a main road is closed, traffic is diverted down side streets. After a stroke, other areas of the brain can sometimes take over some of the work of the damaged area.”

### Keep the design consistent

Use the same fonts, colours and layout throughout. Make text large enough to read from the back of the room, and check that your colours have enough contrast to be read on a projector.

### Rehearse, and record yourself

Practise several times. Record yourself and watch it back: you will notice filler words, rushed sections and awkward transitions you can’t hear while presenting. Rehearse in front of someone outside your field if you can.

### Prepare for questions

Think about the questions you are most likely to be asked, especially the difficult ones, and prepare short answers. A backup slide or two with extra detail can help. If you don’t know an answer, say so, and offer to follow up.

### End by recapping

Restate your main findings and the one thing you want people to remember. Close with a question that keeps them thinking after the talk is over.

For explaining research to people outside your field, see [communicating research to people outside your field](/blog/communicating-research-beyond-your-field).

## In short

A good research presentation is not a summary of your paper read aloud. It is a short, clear story: why the question matters, what you did, what you found and what it means. Keep slides simple, visuals clean and timing tight, rehearse until you can talk to people rather than at a screen, and leave room for questions.
