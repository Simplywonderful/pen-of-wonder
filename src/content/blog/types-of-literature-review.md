There is one step in a research project you can’t afford to skip or rush. Done badly, it can undermine everything that follows. That step is the literature review.

A literature review is a critical synthesis of the earlier research that bears on your topic. It means searching for, assessing and combining what has already been published, so that you understand the current state of knowledge in your field before you try to add to it.

“Literature review” is also a family name. A narrative review and a meta-analysis are both literature reviews, but they answer different questions in very different ways. This guide covers what a review does for your research, then walks through fifteen types, with an example of each.

## What a literature review does

### It establishes context and significance

The review is the foundation your study is built on. By showing what earlier work has found, you show where your research sits in the wider conversation and why it matters.

**Example:** in a study of how climate change affects biodiversity, the review would set out what earlier studies found and point to the areas that still need investigation.

### It reveals gaps and shapes your questions

One of the main jobs of a review is to find gaps, inconsistencies and unanswered questions. When you bring findings from several sources together, contradictions show up, and those contradictions often become your research questions.

**Example:** a review of mindfulness-based therapies for anxiety may turn up conflicting results across studies. That might lead you to investigate which factors explain why the therapy works well for some people and not others.

### It guides your methods

Earlier studies show you which designs, sampling strategies and data collection methods have worked, and where they fell short. You can learn from both before you choose your own.

**Example:** a review of qualitative studies might show how much in-depth interviews add when the aim is to understand people’s own experiences, and help you decide to use them.

### It pulls the evidence into one story

Above all, a review combines findings from many sources into a coherent account. Critical reading and synthesis bring out the themes, patterns and theoretical frameworks that will inform your own work.

**Example:** a review of educational interventions for autistic children might find the same themes in study after study, such as individualised instruction and family involvement, which would then shape how you design your own intervention.

A literature review is not just a summary of what is already known. It is an active process of exploring and discovering.

## Fifteen types of literature review

Review types overlap, and the names are not always used consistently. Grant and Booth’s well-known typology of fourteen review types makes the same point: few of them have fixed, explicit methods, and many are not mutually exclusive ([Grant and Booth, 2009](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1471-1842.2009.00848.x)). Treat the descriptions below as a working guide, and check what your department, supervisor or target journal expects.

### Narrative review

A narrative review gives a broad summary of the research on a topic, drawing out the important findings, trends and debates, and combining them into a readable account of what is known.

**Example:** a narrative review of cognitive behavioural therapy for depression would summarise findings from several studies and discuss their different methods, results and limitations.

### Systematic review

A systematic review searches for, selects and evaluates the literature on a focused question using a method set out in advance. Predefined inclusion and exclusion criteria make the process transparent and repeatable, and reduce bias.

**Example:** a systematic review of vaccination programmes for preventing infectious disease would apply its inclusion and exclusion criteria, then synthesise the included studies, perhaps through meta-analysis or thematic synthesis.

### Meta-analysis

A meta-analysis is a statistical technique. It pools quantitative results from several studies to estimate an overall effect size, and to look for patterns and differences across studies. It is usually carried out as part of a systematic review.

**Example:** a meta-analysis of exercise and mental health would combine data from several trials to estimate how much exercise reduces symptoms of anxiety or depression overall.

### Scoping review

A scoping review maps the literature on a broad topic: the main concepts, the kinds of evidence available and the gaps. It shows the size and shape of a field without necessarily summarising results in detail or rating the quality of each study ([Arksey and O’Malley, 2005](https://doi.org/10.1080/1364557032000119616)).

**Example:** a scoping review of artificial intelligence in healthcare would chart where AI is being used across different areas of care and where more research is needed.

### Critical review

A critical review goes beyond summary. It examines the key ideas, claims and methods in a body of work and assesses their strengths, weaknesses and implications.

**Example:** a critical review of theories of motivation in organisational psychology would assess the theory, the evidence and the practical implications of each approach, and show where the field agrees and disagrees.

### Rapid review

A rapid review is a streamlined review used when evidence is needed quickly. It simplifies or limits some steps of a systematic review, for example by narrowing the search, and is open about what it left out.

**Example:** early in the COVID-19 pandemic, a rapid review of new treatments could bring the emerging evidence together quickly enough to inform clinical and policy decisions.

### Integrative review

An integrative review combines evidence from different kinds of research, including experimental and non-experimental studies and different theoretical perspectives, to build a fuller understanding of a complex topic or a new conceptual model ([Whittemore and Knafl, 2005](https://doi.org/10.1111/j.1365-2648.2005.03621.x)).

**Example:** an integrative review of what affects patients’ adherence to their medication might draw on clinical, psychological and sociological studies to find common themes and underlying mechanisms.

### Argumentative review

An argumentative review gathers and examines the literature in support of a clear thesis. It states a position and builds a case for it from the evidence. Because it sets out to argue, it should still deal honestly with the evidence against its position.

**Example:** an argumentative review of sustainability in the fashion industry might draw on empirical data, ethical arguments and financial incentives to make the case for sustainable production methods.

### Descriptive review

A descriptive review summarises what research on a topic has found and how, without going far into synthesis or critique. It is useful when you need a basic understanding of a field.

**Example:** a descriptive review of educational interventions for children with learning difficulties would set out the methods, findings and limitations of earlier studies, as groundwork for further research.

### Umbrella review

An umbrella review brings together the findings of several systematic reviews and meta-analyses on a broad question, a review of reviews ([Aromataris and colleagues, 2015](https://doi.org/10.1097/XEB.0000000000000055)). It shows where the reviews agree and where they conflict.

**Example:** an umbrella review of dietary interventions for weight management would compare the conclusions of systematic reviews and meta-analyses of different diets.

### Theoretical review

A theoretical review examines and summarises the theories, concepts and models relevant to a topic. It asks how a phenomenon has been understood and explained, rather than what any one study found.

**Example:** a theoretical review of resilience in psychology might compare the ways resilience has been defined, the models proposed and the ways it is measured.

### Mixed methods review

A mixed methods review combines qualitative and quantitative evidence in one synthesis, which makes it useful for complex problems that need more than one kind of evidence. A large review of reviews of this kind found two main synthesis designs: convergent, where the two kinds of evidence are brought together in the same stage of analysis, and sequential, where one synthesis informs the next ([Hong and colleagues, 2017](https://doi.org/10.1186/s13643-017-0454-2)).

**Example:** a mixed methods review of the barriers marginalised communities face in getting healthcare might combine survey data on how often people miss care with interview studies on why.

### Thematic review

A thematic review identifies and examines the themes that recur across a body of literature, and combines findings around them to offer new perspectives or frameworks.

**Example:** a thematic review of student engagement in higher education might identify themes such as motivation, autonomy and belonging, and examine how each relates to learning and achievement.

### State-of-the-art review

A state-of-the-art review concentrates on the most recent work in a field: new developments, current approaches and the directions research is likely to take next.

**Example:** a state-of-the-art review of AI in healthcare would look at recent advances, clinical applications and ethical issues, which would help both practitioners and researchers.

### Annotated bibliography

An annotated bibliography is not a review in the full sense, but it is often the first step towards one. It is a selected list of sources on a topic, each with a short note on its main findings, methods and contribution, and often a brief assessment of its quality and relevance.

**Example:** an annotated bibliography on environmental sustainability in urban planning might include policy papers and peer-reviewed studies, each with a short summary and a note on how useful it is.

## How to choose

Your research question, your objectives and your methods decide which type of review you need. A few questions help:

- **Do you need a precise answer to a focused question?** A systematic review, perhaps with a meta-analysis, is the usual route.
- **Do you need to know what exists in a broad or new field?** A scoping review fits.
- **Do you need evidence fast for a decision?** A rapid review, with its shortcuts stated openly.
- **Do you need to bring different kinds of evidence together?** An integrative or mixed methods review.
- **Are you writing the literature section of a thesis or paper?** Most of these are narrative, thematic or critical reviews, built around your research question.

Whatever you choose, name it accurately and follow its method. A review that calls itself systematic but doesn’t report its search, its criteria and how studies were selected will not survive peer review, and it shouldn’t.
