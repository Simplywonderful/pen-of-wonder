This is the second half of my guide to structuring a research paper. [Part 1](/blog/structuring-a-research-paper-introduction-literature-methods) covered the overall shape of a paper, then the introduction, the literature review and the methodology. This part covers the rest: the results and discussion, the summary, conclusion and recommendations, and the references.

As in part 1, the quoted passages are written as they might appear in a paper. The studies, authors and figures in them are invented for illustration, not real research to cite.

## Results and discussion

Some journals want results and discussion as separate sections; others combine them. Check your target journal’s author guidelines. Either way, the two jobs are different. The results say what you found. The discussion says what it means.

### Presenting your results

**Be clear and concise.** Use tables, figures and charts where they make the findings easier to see, and refer to each one in the text with a clear label, so the reader knows what to look at and why.

> “Self-esteem scores were lower among participants who reported more daily social media use (r = −0.32, p < 0.01). Figure 1 shows the distribution of self-esteem scores among high, moderate and low users.”

**Don’t overload the reader.** Give enough detail to support your findings, but concentrate on the results that answer your research questions. Extra analyses can go in an appendix or supplementary file.

> “Although we collected data on many aspects of social media use, this section focuses on frequency of use and its association with self-esteem, the primary variables of interest.”

**Report, don’t interpret.** Keep the results section to what you found. If you start explaining why, you are writing the discussion.

### Interpreting your findings

**Put your results in context.** Show how your findings agree with or differ from earlier research. This is where your study’s contribution becomes visible.

> “Our findings are consistent with Author A and colleagues (2020), who also reported a negative association between social media use and self-esteem. Our study extends this by comparing different types of platform.”

**Explore the implications.** Say what your findings add to knowledge in your field, and what they might mean in practice. Keep the claims in proportion to the evidence: a correlation in a cross-sectional survey shows an association, not that one thing caused the other.

> “The association between heavier social media use and lower self-esteem suggests that helping adolescents develop healthier social media habits may be worth testing. Educators and parents may want to be aware of these patterns.”

**Address the limitations and future research.** State the limitations of your study honestly and suggest what future research should do about them. Reviewers trust a paper that knows its own weaknesses.

> “This study relied on self-reported data, which may be subject to bias. Future research could follow participants over time and use objective measures of social media use, which would give stronger evidence about the direction of the relationship.”

## Summary, conclusion and recommendations

This section is your last chance to show the reader why your research matters. It summarises what you found, draws out its importance and points to what should happen next. Journals differ in what they call this section and how long they want it, so check the guidelines.

### A summary that adds something

**Recap concisely.** Give a short overview of the research problem, the method, the key findings and their application. It should not simply repeat the abstract.

> “This study examined the association between social media use and adolescent self-esteem. Using a mixed methods design, we found that more frequent use was associated with lower self-esteem, particularly on image-based platforms.”

**Avoid repetition.** The earlier sections reported the detail, including the statistics. The summary should pull the main thread together in fresh words, not copy sentences from the results.

### Conclusions that connect

**Synthesise the findings.** Connect your findings to the wider research and say what your work contributes.

> “Our findings support calls for programmes that help adolescents use social media in healthier ways, and add to the evidence on the psychological effects of online interaction.”

**Return to your objectives.** Revisit the aims and objectives you set out in the introduction, and show how your findings address each one. If an objective was only partly met, say so.

> “The study met its objective of assessing the association between social media use and self-esteem, and the findings are consistent with our initial hypothesis.”

### Recommendations

**Make them practical.** Base your recommendations on your findings, and say who could act on them: policymakers, practitioners or other stakeholders.

> “Schools could consider digital literacy programmes that encourage balanced social media use.”

**Point to future research.** Say what still needs investigating. This acknowledges the limits of your study and adds to the field’s agenda.

> “Future research could follow adolescents over several years, compare specific platforms and include a wider range of demographic groups.”

## References

The reference list is easy to treat as an afterthought, but it is far more than a list of sources. It shows the range and depth of your reading, gives credit to the work you built on, and lets readers check your evidence for themselves.

### Accuracy and completeness

Every source you cite in the text must appear in the reference list, and every entry in the list must be cited in the text. A mismatch is one of the first things a careful reviewer notices.

If you cite Smith (2020) in your paper, the full entry for Smith (2020) must be in your references.

### Consistent formatting

Use one citation style throughout, exactly as your journal or university specifies. That means consistent capitalisation, punctuation, italics and order of information in every entry.

The most common styles:

- **APA** (American Psychological Association), now in its 7th edition, is widely used in psychology, education and the social sciences.
- **MLA** (Modern Language Association), now in its 9th edition, is common in the humanities.
- **Chicago**, now in its 18th edition, is used in history and some social sciences.
- **Harvard** is widely used in UK universities and many other fields. There is no single official version: many UK universities follow *Cite Them Right*, others publish their own guide. Use the version your institution or journal names.

Here is the same invented book and journal article in each style. The details are illustrative, so check every entry against your style’s current manual or your library’s guide.

**APA 7th edition**

- Book: Smith, J. (2020). *Understanding human behaviour*. Academic Press.
- Article: Doe, J. (2021). The impact of social media on youth. *Journal of Social Research*, *12*(3), 45–67.

**MLA 9th edition**

- Book: Smith, John. *Understanding Human Behaviour*. Academic Press, 2020.
- Article: Doe, Jane. “The Impact of Social Media on Youth.” *Journal of Social Research*, vol. 12, no. 3, 2021, pp. 45–67.

**Chicago 18th edition (bibliography)**

- Book: Smith, John. *Understanding Human Behaviour*. Academic Press, 2020.
- Article: Doe, Jane. “The Impact of Social Media on Youth.” *Journal of Social Research* 12, no. 3 (2021): 45–67.

**Harvard (Cite Them Right)**

- Book: Smith, J. (2020) *Understanding human behaviour*. 2nd edn. New York: Academic Press.
- Article: Doe, J. (2021) ‘The impact of social media on youth’, *Journal of Social Research*, 12(3), pp. 45–67.

Where a source has a DOI, most current styles ask you to include it as a link at the end of the entry.

### Proper attribution

Citing your sources gives other researchers credit for their work and protects you from plagiarism. It also lets readers follow up your sources, and makes your own argument more credible.

When you use an idea from a source, attribute it to the original author, whether you quote or paraphrase: “According to Smith (2020), human behaviour is influenced by many factors.”

### Use a reference manager

Reference managers such as Zotero, EndNote and Mendeley Reference Manager store your sources and format citations and reference lists in your chosen style automatically. They save a great deal of time, but they are only as good as the details you put in, so check each entry, especially titles, capitalisation and DOIs.

### Keep up with the rules

Citation styles are updated from time to time, so follow the current edition. For example, APA 7th edition changed the rule for in-text citations of works with three or more authors: you now use the first author’s name and “et al.” from the first citation. Write (Smith et al., 2020), not (Smith, Brown, & Johnson, 2020).

### Double-check before you submit

Before submission, go through your references one last time. Check that every entry is accurate and complete, that the list matches your in-text citations exactly, and that the formatting is consistent from the first entry to the last.

## In short

Present your results clearly and keep interpretation for the discussion. Interpret your findings in the light of earlier research, honestly and without overclaiming. End with a summary, conclusion and recommendations that tie back to your objectives and point forward. And treat the reference list with the same care as the rest of the paper: it is the evidence that your argument stands on.
