Imagine you have spent months, perhaps years, collecting your data. The analysis is finished. Now comes a step that is crucial and often rushed: working out what your findings actually mean.

This is where data becomes something people can use, in practice, in policy and in future research. It is also where many studies go wrong, not because the analysis was faulty, but because the interpretation claimed more than the data could support.

This guide covers what interpretation involves, the most common mistakes, and the habits that keep it accurate. The examples are illustrations, not real studies.

## What interpreting your findings means

Interpreting your findings means working out what your results mean for your research question and for your field. It goes beyond reporting statistics or noting what is statistically significant. It takes critical thinking: recognising patterns, judging how much the results matter, and drawing conclusions you can defend.

Five things are at the heart of it.

### 1. Context

Start by placing your results alongside earlier research. Where do they agree with what others have found, and where do they differ?

**Example:** if a study of a new teaching method finds a clear improvement in student performance, compare it with earlier studies of similar methods. Is the improvement in line with theirs, larger or smaller? If it differs, why might that be?

### 2. Practical importance, not just statistical significance

Statistical significance and practical importance are not the same thing. A statistically significant result only tells you the finding is unlikely to be due to chance alone, under the assumptions of the test. It doesn’t tell you how large or how important the effect is. The American Statistical Association makes this point in its statement on p-values: a p-value does not measure the size of an effect or the importance of a result ([Wasserstein and Lazar, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1080/00031305.2016.1154108)).

**Example:** a new drug might produce a statistically significant reduction in symptoms in a large trial, but if the reduction is very small, it may make little difference to patients. Report the effect size and its confidence interval, and say plainly what the difference means in practice.

### 3. Limitations

Every study has limitations: in its sample, its design, its measures or its setting. Acknowledging them gives your interpretation more authority, not less.

**Example:** a study of workplace efficiency carried out in one company may not apply to other companies, let alone other industries. Say so.

### 4. Implications for practice and policy

Ask what your findings mean for the people who could use them. What do they suggest to practitioners in your field? What might policymakers take from them? What should researchers study next?

**Example:** a study of urban air pollution might inform new regulations, or point to which ways of reducing pollution most need testing.

### 5. A clear story

Results are easier to understand and remember when they are part of a clear account: the problem, what you found and why it matters. A well-chosen example or case can help readers see what a finding means for real people.

Use stories to illustrate your evidence, never to replace it. A vivid case that is typical of your data helps the reader. One that is chosen because it is striking, when most of your data says something different, misleads them.

## The mistakes to avoid

### Ignoring the wider context

Research doesn’t happen in a vacuum. It builds on earlier findings and adds to an ongoing conversation. Interpreting your results as if nothing else had been done is one of the most serious mistakes you can make.

**Example:** a study finds that remote workers’ output rose by 20%. Without comparing that with similar studies, and without considering outside factors such as the changes in working life during the COVID-19 pandemic, the figure could easily mislead.

### Confusing statistical significance with practical importance

As above: a significant result can be trivially small. If a drug lowers symptoms by half a percentage point, “statistically significant” may be true and still not worth much to a patient.

### Ignoring bias and limitations

Overlook your study’s limitations and you will overgeneralise. If everyone who answered your job satisfaction survey works in one industry, your findings may not hold in others, and your interpretation needs to say so.

### Treating correlation as causation

That two things move together doesn’t mean one causes the other.

**Example:** income and education are positively correlated, but that alone doesn’t show that getting a degree will raise a particular person’s salary. Other factors, such as industry, experience and family background, play a part, and some of them may affect both.

### Letting the story outrun the data

A good story helps people care about your findings. But an anecdote or testimonial is not evidence of a trend. If you illustrate a finding about remote work with one person’s experience, make sure the data supports the general point, and say how typical the example is.

### Highlighting only the positive results

Research often emphasises positive results and passes over negative or null findings. That distorts the picture.

**Example:** if a new teaching method improved results in three of ten schools and made no difference in the other seven, reporting only the three successes would badly mislead anyone deciding whether to adopt it. Report what didn’t work as clearly as what did.

## Habits that keep your interpretation honest

### Keep your research question in front of you

Let your original question guide your interpretation. If your study is about social media and academic performance, focus on what your findings say about that, however interesting the side roads in your data may be. Genuinely unexpected findings can be reported, but label them as exploratory.

### Compare your findings with earlier work

Say whether your results agree or disagree with earlier studies, and where they differ, suggest why: differences in sample, setting, measures or time.

### Use the right statistical methods, and know their limits

Misapplied statistics lead to misleading conclusions. Check that your methods suit your data and your question. Regression, for example, can adjust for confounding variables you have measured, which strengthens your interpretation. It can’t account for confounders you didn’t measure, so it can’t by itself rule out other explanations.

### Be open about limitations

State clearly whether your sample was small or unrepresentative, and what that means for how far your findings apply. Openness sets realistic expectations and builds trust.

### Bring in qualitative insight

Numbers can show that something is happening; qualitative data can help explain why. If your survey shows a striking trend, follow-up interviews or case studies can explore the reasons behind it.

### Visualise your data

A good chart can make a complex result clear at a glance. Choose the chart that suits the data: a scatter plot for a relationship, a bar chart for comparing groups, a heat map to show where values cluster.

### Ask others to challenge you

Share your interpretation with colleagues before you publish, at a seminar, a lab meeting or a workshop. Fresh eyes spot blind spots and biases you can’t see yourself.

## In short

Good interpretation sets your results in context, separates what is statistically significant from what matters, admits its limitations, avoids claiming cause from correlation, and reports the disappointing results as well as the good ones. Tell a clear story, but make sure the data can carry it. A conclusion that claims a little less, and holds up, is worth far more than one that claims too much.
