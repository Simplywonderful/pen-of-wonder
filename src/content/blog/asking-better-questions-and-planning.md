In 2025 I shared thirty lessons from my years as a researcher, one at a time. This piece brings together five of them about the groundwork: asking the right question, using what you already have, outlining, planning your time and keeping your research organised. None of it is glamorous, but it is what makes the rest of the work possible.

## Lesson 5: Your next breakthrough might be in your old notes

As researchers, we are wired to keep looking for the next source, the next dataset, the next lead.

But sometimes the answers are already in our hands.

Here is what I mean:

- **Revisit your old notes.** You may see patterns you missed the first time.
- **Follow the citation trails** in papers you have already read. Their reference lists, and the newer papers that cite them, often lead straight to what you need.
- **Re-analyse data you already have.** Asking new questions of an old dataset can produce fresh insights.

> **Pro tip:** before you re-analyse existing data, check that your ethics approval, consent forms and any data-sharing agreement cover the new question. If they don’t, ask your ethics committee or the data owner first.

The best discoveries aren’t always found by digging deeper. Sometimes they are found by looking again.

## Lesson 6: The quality of your questions decides the quality of your research

Good research starts with good questions.

Early in my research career, I often felt stuck. Not because I lacked information, but because I wasn’t asking the right questions.

In research, vague questions lead to vague answers. Specific, well-structured questions lead to clear, precise answers. The wrong question keeps you going in circles; the right one points you towards exactly what you need.

Here is my process for improving a research question:

- **Start broad** to understand the landscape.
- **Narrow down** by defining your variables, your timeframe and your context.
- **Test it.** Could someone misunderstand the question? If so, refine it.

For example, “How does social media affect students?” is a starting point, not a research question. “Is daily time on social media associated with exam performance among first-year undergraduates at one university?” names the variables, the group and the setting, and it is clear what an answer would look like.

A well-crafted question works like a laser. It cuts through the noise to what you need to know.

## Lesson 7: Start with a simple outline

When I used to skip outlining, writing felt like walking through a maze, full of twists, turns and doubling back. I would write in circles, repeat points and miss key arguments.

My writing changed the day I started outlining.

An outline is more than a list. It is your map. It helps you:

- see the big picture before you start;
- organise your arguments in a logical order;
- spot the gaps early, while they are still easy to fix.

It doesn’t have to be perfect. Start messy and refine it as you go. But have one.

You will spend less time stuck and more time producing work that means something.

## Lesson 19: Plan your timeline backwards

Deadlines feel far away, until they don’t.

In academia, deadlines are rarely negotiable. The secret to meeting them is working backwards.

Start from the due date and map each milestone in reverse:

- revisions;
- writing the draft;
- analysis;
- data collection.

Give each stage a date, and you will know exactly when each step needs to be done. If the dates don’t fit, you find out now, while you can still change the plan, rather than the week before submission.

> **Pro tip:** build in time for the things you don’t control, such as ethics approval, recruitment and waiting for feedback from your supervisor or co-authors. They almost always take longer than you expect.

Planning backwards prevents last-minute panic and keeps your progress steady.

## Lesson 29: Organise, or be overwhelmed

If your research files are scattered across folders, drives and email attachments, you are setting yourself up for chaos.

I used to waste hours looking for one elusive PDF I knew I had saved. Then I built a system. Now every note, article, dataset and draft has a home, and the mental clarity is worth a great deal.

To organise your research:

- **Use a reference manager** such as Zotero, EndNote or Mendeley Reference Manager. It stores your sources and their PDFs in one place, and formats your citations for you.
- **Build a folder structure** for each project, with separate places for drafts, data and sources, and name files consistently so you can tell versions apart.
- **Keep a “parking lot” document** for ideas that don’t fit anywhere yet. They will be there when you need them.

Your research is only as strong as the system that supports it.

## What ties these together

All five lessons are about preparation. Ask a precise question, make full use of what you already have, outline before you write, plan backwards from the deadline and keep your materials where you can find them. Do that, and the hard parts of research, the thinking, the analysis and the writing, get the time and attention they deserve.
