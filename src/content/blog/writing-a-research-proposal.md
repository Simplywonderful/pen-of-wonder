An architect sketches every detail of a building before anyone lays a foundation. A research proposal does the same job for a study. It sets out what you will investigate, how, and why it matters, before you collect a single piece of data.

A proposal can feel like a hurdle between you and the “real” research. It is better to treat it as the foundation of the whole project. A clear proposal gets approved faster, and it keeps you on course when the research gets complicated.

This guide covers what a proposal must show, how to structure it, and the mistakes that most often send proposals back for revision.

## What a research proposal is

A proposal, in general, is a formal plan put forward for others to consider: for a project, a business or a piece of research.

A **research proposal** is a detailed plan that sets out the objectives, methods and significance of a study you intend to carry out. It does two jobs. It guides your own research, and it persuades others (a supervisor, an ethics committee, a funder or an admissions panel) to approve or fund it.

## Part 1: Five things a proposal must show

### 1. Clear research questions

Start by stating exactly what you want to find out. What are you investigating, and in whom, where and when? Write your questions precisely, and make sure they fit the scope and objectives of the study. Everything else in the proposal follows from them.

### 2. A real grasp of the literature

Review the existing research on your topic thoroughly. Show what is already known, and point to the gaps, inconsistencies or unanswered questions your study will address. The literature review is where you prove you understand the field well enough to add to it.

### 3. Methods that are sound and feasible

Describe the methods you plan to use, and explain why they suit your questions. Then check they are realistic. Can you do this within your time, budget and access to participants or data? A rigorous method you can’t complete is not a good method for this proposal.

### 4. Why the study matters

Say clearly what your research will contribute. How does it fill a gap or move knowledge forward? Who could use the findings: practitioners, policymakers, other researchers? Reviewers need to see the point of the study, not just its design.

### 5. Ethics and risk

Explain how you will protect participants’ rights, obtain informed consent, keep data confidential and secure, and handle any risks or adverse events. Ethics is not a box to tick at the end; committees will read this section closely.

## Part 2: Structuring the proposal

A well-structured proposal does more than look tidy. It makes your plan easy to follow, shows reviewers you can organise a project, and makes it easier for them to give you useful feedback.

Your university or funder may set its own template, and if so, follow it exactly. Most proposals cover the same ground in roughly this order.

### Title and abstract

- **Title:** short and specific. It should say what the study is about without unnecessary words.
- **Abstract:** a brief summary of your aims, methods and significance, so a reviewer knows within a minute what you plan to do and why.

### Introduction

- Give the background to the topic and the purpose of the study.
- State your research questions or objectives and the scope of the project.

### Literature review

- Analyse the existing research on your question, rather than listing it.
- Identify the gaps, inconsistencies or areas needing further work, and show where your study fits.

### Methodology

- Explain your study design, sampling, data collection methods and analysis.
- Justify each choice: why this design, this sample, this method of analysis, for these questions.

### Significance and contribution

- Explain how the research could advance knowledge, policy or practice.
- Be specific about what will be different once the study is done.

### Ethical considerations

- Discuss participant selection, informed consent, data privacy and possible risks.
- Set out how you will protect participants’ rights and welfare.

### Timeline and resources

- Many proposals also need a timeline and a list of resources or a budget. Work backwards from your deadline and plan each stage: ethics approval, data collection, analysis, writing and revision.

### Word count

Respect the word limit. Going over it can cost marks or get the proposal returned, and staying within it forces you to be clear.

## Part 3: Five common mistakes

### 1. Unclear objectives

If the objectives aren’t stated precisely, reviewers can’t tell what the study is for.

**Example:** “To investigate the impact of social media on mental health.” Whose mental health? Which aspects of social media? Measured how, and over what period? A clearer version names the population, the variables and the outcome: “To examine the association between daily hours of social media use and symptoms of anxiety among undergraduates at one Nigerian university.”

### 2. A thin literature review

A superficial review undermines the whole proposal. Leaving out important or recent studies suggests you don’t yet know the field, and it can mean the gap you claim has already been filled.

### 3. Vague methods

Ambiguity in the methods makes reviewers doubt the study can be done properly.

**Example:** a proposal that says it will use “qualitative methods” without saying which (interviews? focus groups? document analysis?), with whom, how many, or how the data will be analysed.

### 4. Ignoring ethics

Leaving out ethical issues puts participants and researchers at risk, and it is one of the quickest ways to have a proposal sent back.

**Example:** a plan that never says how informed consent will be obtained or how participants’ data will be kept confidential.

### 5. Careless presentation

Spelling mistakes, inconsistent formatting and disorganised sections make a proposal harder to read and less convincing. Reviewers may reasonably wonder whether the research will be done with the same care.

## In short

A good proposal answers four questions clearly: what you will study, how, why it matters, and how you will do it ethically. Structure it so a reviewer can follow your plan, check every section against the mistakes above, and treat it as the foundation of your research rather than a formality. A proposal you understand well enough to defend line by line is a proposal you can actually carry out.
