Qualitative data analysis is where participants’ stories come to life, and words build a picture.

Quantitative analysis looks for patterns and relationships in numbers, using statistical techniques. Qualitative analysis explores meanings, patterns and themes in text, images and audio. The data can take many forms: interview transcripts, field notes, documents, audio recordings, photographs and answers to open-ended survey questions.

This guide covers three common techniques, the practices that make qualitative findings trustworthy, and the mistakes to avoid.

## Part 1: Three common techniques

### Thematic analysis

Thematic analysis is one of the most widely used ways of analysing qualitative data. It identifies themes, patterns of meaning, across a dataset. You code the data systematically, then group codes into themes that answer your research question. Braun and Clarke’s paper on using thematic analysis in psychology is a widely used guide to doing it, and a good place to start ([Braun and Clarke, 2006](https://doi.org/10.1191/1478088706qp063oa)).

It works with most kinds of qualitative data, including interview transcripts and open-ended survey answers.

**Example:** in a study of how people experience stress at work, themes might include workload, relationships with colleagues and ways of coping.

### Grounded theory

Grounded theory aims to build a theory, or a conceptual framework, from the data itself, rather than testing one decided in advance. You collect and analyse data in cycles: early analysis shapes what data you collect next, and the emerging concepts are refined until they explain what is going on.

It is especially useful for studying complex social processes, or areas where existing theory doesn’t fit well.

**Example:** a study of organisational change might use interviews and observations with staff to build a model of how the change unfolded and how people responded to it.

### Content analysis

Content analysis examines text or visual material systematically to identify patterns, themes and meanings. It can be more or less quantitative. Some approaches derive categories directly from the data; others start from categories based on theory or earlier research; and some count words or content, then interpret what the counts mean ([Hsieh and Shannon, 2005](https://doi.org/10.1177/1049732305276687)).

It suits documents, media coverage, social media posts, images and other material that already exists.

**Example:** to study how climate change is portrayed in the media, you might analyse newspaper articles for recurring themes and ways of framing the issue.

## Part 2: Practices that make your analysis trustworthy

### Practise reflexivity

Reflexivity means thinking critically about your own role, assumptions and possible biases as a researcher, and how they shape your analysis. Acknowledging them openly makes your findings more credible, not less.

Build it into your routine. Keep a reflexive journal, and talk your interpretations through with colleagues.

**Example:** if you are a health professional studying patients’ experiences of care, consider how your own experience in the field shapes what you notice in the data and how you interpret it.

### Show that your findings can be trusted

Qualitative research is judged by different criteria from quantitative research. A widely used set comes from Lincoln and Guba’s 1985 book *Naturalistic Inquiry*: credibility, transferability, dependability and confirmability. Several techniques help:

- **Triangulation:** using more than one data source, method or researcher to see whether the findings hold up.
- **Member checking:** going back to participants to check your account of what they said. It can add credibility, but treat it with care: participants may not recognise themselves in a higher-level analysis, and disagreement doesn’t automatically mean your interpretation is wrong ([Birt and colleagues, 2016](https://doi.org/10.1177/1049732316654870)).
- **Peer debriefing:** asking colleagues or experts to question your interpretations.

**Example:** in a study of how students experience online learning, you might compare what you find in interviews with survey data and classroom observations.

### Be transparent

Record and report your decisions: how you sampled, how you collected the data, and how you analysed it, step by step. An audit trail lets readers see how you got from the raw data to your themes, and judge the quality of the work for themselves.

Qualitative studies aren’t usually expected to be repeated exactly, as an experiment might be. But readers need to be able to follow your reasoning.

**Example:** in a study of how a community views local pollution, you would describe exactly how participants were recruited, how interviews were conducted and how the themes were developed.

## Part 3: Mistakes to avoid

### Overlooking reflexivity

Ignoring your own role in the research can narrow your perspective and bias your interpretation without your noticing.

**Example:** a researcher who has worked in healthcare, studying patients’ experiences, may unconsciously read the data through their own professional assumptions.

### Treating saturation as a rule you can’t question

Saturation is the point at which new data stops producing new insights. The idea comes from grounded theory, where it is called theoretical saturation, and many qualitative studies use some version of it to decide when they have collected enough data ([Saunders and colleagues, 2018](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11135-017-0574-8)).

Two mistakes are common. One is stopping too early, before the analysis has explored the topic properly, and drawing premature conclusions. The other is claiming “saturation” without saying what it meant or how you judged it. Not every approach uses saturation at all: Braun and Clarke argue it doesn’t fit reflexive thematic analysis, where decisions about how much data is enough are a matter of judgement made during the analysis ([Braun and Clarke, 2021](https://doi.org/10.1080/2159676X.2019.1704846)). Whatever approach you use, explain how you decided you had enough data.

### Ignoring context

Context shapes meaning. If you don’t take account of the social, cultural, historical and political setting of your data, your analysis can end up shallow or simply wrong. Place your findings in the setting they came from.

### Relying on software to do the thinking

Qualitative analysis software such as NVivo is useful for organising, coding, searching and visualising data. But it supports your analysis; it doesn’t do it. The software won’t find your themes or interpret your data. Over-reliance on it can crowd out the reflection, creativity and critical thinking that good qualitative analysis depends on.

## In short

Choose the technique that fits your question: thematic analysis for patterns of meaning, grounded theory for building theory, content analysis for systematic study of existing material. Be reflexive, show why your findings can be trusted, and make every step transparent. Avoid the common mistakes, and use software as a tool, not a substitute for thinking. Done well, qualitative analysis shows the depth and richness in your data.
