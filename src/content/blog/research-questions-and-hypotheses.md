Most research projects begin with something that looks simple: a question. That question then becomes the basis of the whole study. It decides what you read, what data you collect, how you analyse it and what you can claim at the end.

This guide covers how to write a good research question, how to develop a hypothesis from it, and how the two should fit together. The examples are illustrations I have written for this guide, not real studies.

## Part 1: Research questions

A research question is more than a query. It sets the goal, the scope and the direction of your study. A good one is focused enough to answer, and interesting enough to be worth answering.

### Be specific, and fit the field

Everyone says a research question must be specific, and it must. But think, too, about how it fits the wider field. A good question connects to the current literature, and still reflects the curiosity that led you to it in the first place.

### Avoid ambiguity

An ambiguous question is a mirage: it looks like a destination, but you can never reach it. Make sure every key term has one clear meaning and the limits of the question are stated, so nobody, including you, can misread what the study is for.

### Turn vague into precise

Vague questions always produce vague answers. Define your terms, set the scope, and keep refining the question until it is clear.

**Example:**

- **Vague:** How does social media affect students?
- **Better:** How is social media use related to academic performance among university students?
- **Precise:** Is the number of hours spent on social media each day associated with end-of-year grades among first-year undergraduates at one Nigerian university?

The precise version names the variables, the population and the setting. You can see what data would answer it.

### Expect it to develop

Research rarely goes exactly to plan. Your question may sharpen as you read the literature and plan your methods. That is normal, and a well-framed question leaves room for it. Once data collection starts, though, record any change and the reason for it, so your final report is honest about how the question evolved.

### Balance novelty and feasibility

Originality matters, but so does whether you can actually answer the question. Weigh what you would like to find out against the time, money, access and methods you have. A brilliant question you can’t complete is not the right question for this project.

### Leave room for discovery

Not every question needs to predict an answer. Open-ended questions, especially in qualitative research, invite exploration and can lead to findings you didn’t expect.

**Example:** “How do first-year students describe the role social media plays in their study habits?” is open-ended. It asks what is happening, not whether a predicted relationship exists.

### Let the question come before the hypothesis

Your research question is the ground your hypotheses grow from. Don’t narrow the question too early just to fit a hypothesis you already have in mind. Get the question right first.

## Part 2: Hypotheses

A hypothesis is not a wild guess, or even simply an educated one. It is a clear, testable statement of the relationship you expect to find between variables. It is the core of the investigation in most quantitative studies, and it sets up how you will test that relationship.

### Know the kinds of hypothesis

Most students learn about the **null hypothesis** (there is no relationship or difference) and the **alternative hypothesis** (there is one). But there is a further choice to make. A **non-directional** hypothesis predicts a relationship without saying which way it goes. A **directional** hypothesis predicts the direction too, which makes a sharper claim.

**Example:**

- **Null:** There is no association between daily hours of social media use and end-of-year grades.
- **Non-directional:** There is an association between daily hours of social media use and end-of-year grades.
- **Directional:** Students who spend more hours a day on social media have lower end-of-year grades.

Use a directional hypothesis only when theory or earlier evidence gives you good reason to expect that direction, and decide before you see the data.

### Take account of context

Hypotheses don’t exist in isolation. Social, cultural, economic and historical context can all affect the variables you are studying. A relationship found in one country, or one decade, may not hold in another, so let what you know of your setting shape what you predict.

### Hypotheses in qualitative research

Formal hypothesis testing belongs mainly to quantitative research. Qualitative studies usually start from open research questions instead, and many of them *generate* hypotheses or theory from the data, as grounded theory does, rather than test hypotheses set in advance. If your study is qualitative, you may not need a hypothesis at all. Check what your design and your department expect.

### Revise before you test, not after

Your hypothesis can and should improve while you are planning: as you read, refine your question and choose your measures. Once you have seen the results, though, changing the hypothesis to match them and presenting it as if it had been your prediction all along is a well-known problem, called HARKing, or hypothesising after the results are known ([Kerr, 1998](https://doi.org/10.1207/s15327957pspr0203_4)). It makes chance findings look like confirmed predictions.

If the data suggest a new idea, report it honestly as exploratory, and as a hypothesis for future research to test. Writing your hypotheses down before you collect data, or pre-registering them, makes this easy to show.

### Make sure you can measure it

A hypothesis is only testable if you can measure its variables. Check that your instruments can actually capture what your hypothesis talks about, and think about their limits. “Students who use social media more have lower wellbeing” is only testable once you have decided how you will measure both use and wellbeing.

### Balance specificity and generality

A good hypothesis is specific enough to test and general enough to matter. Too narrow, and the result tells you little beyond your own sample. Too broad, and you can’t design a test that would show it wrong.

## Part 3: How questions and hypotheses fit together

### Questions come first

You start with the questions that interest you, and let them develop as you read and plan. Once the questions are clear, hypotheses follow from them logically.

**Example:** the question “Is daily social media use associated with end-of-year grades among first-year undergraduates?” leads directly to the directional hypothesis above.

### Keep them aligned

The question sets the framework; the hypotheses build on it. Every hypothesis should answer part of a research question, and every question that calls for a prediction should have one. If a hypothesis doesn’t connect to any of your questions, either the hypothesis or the questions need rethinking.

### Expect them to inform each other

Questions and hypotheses develop together. Working out a hypothesis can show you that your question needs sharpening, and the answers to one study’s questions raise the questions for the next. Within a single study, keep that refinement to the planning stage, for the reasons above.

### Balance breadth and depth

Questions tend to cover broad ground. Hypotheses focus on specific relationships. Used together, they give you a study that is both wide enough to matter and focused enough to produce clear answers.

### Tell one story

Your questions and hypotheses should read like chapters of the same book. Each one adds to the overall argument of your research, and together they lead the reader from what you wanted to know to what you found.

## In short

Write a question that is specific, unambiguous and feasible. If your design calls for one, turn it into a hypothesis you can measure and test, and fix it before you see the data. Keep your questions and hypotheses aligned so they tell one clear story. Get these right, and every later decision in your study has something firm to follow.
