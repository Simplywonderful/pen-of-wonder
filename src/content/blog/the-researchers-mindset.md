In 2025 I shared thirty lessons from my years as a researcher, one at a time. This piece brings together the last nine of them. They are less about methods and more about the person doing the research: how you think, how you keep going, and how you grow.

## Lesson 12: Uncertainty is part of the process

When I began my research career, I thought a “real” academic was someone who knew everything about their topic.

One of the biggest lessons I’ve learned is that you don’t have to know everything to be a good researcher.

Research isn’t about being a walking encyclopaedia. It is about curiosity, persistence and knowing where to look. It is about discovery, and discovery means living with uncertainty.

The best researchers don’t have all the answers. They have the mindset to keep asking better questions.

It’s fine to say, “I don’t know yet.” What matters is that you know how to find out.

Give yourself permission to grow into your expertise. It takes time, and it isn’t a performance.

## Lesson 18: Treat feedback as a gift

Feedback used to sting. I dreaded reviewers’ comments.

Now I see them as a shortcut to improvement, something like a free coaching session for my work.

Good feedback:

- shows you your blind spots;
- challenges weak arguments;
- offers perspectives you hadn’t considered.

It isn’t personal; it’s professional. Use feedback as a tool, not a verdict. The right feedback doesn’t just fix mistakes. It strengthens the whole project.

If your reviews have just come back, my guide to [responding to reviewer comments](/blog/reviewer-feedback-peer-review-cover-letters) shows how to answer them point by point.

## Lesson 20: Feeling like a fraud? You’re not alone

If you have ever felt you’re not good enough to be in academia, you are far from alone. Even accomplished researchers sometimes feel they don’t belong.

Impostor feelings are common, especially in settings full of high achievers. A systematic review of the research found them reported across many professions, though estimates of how common they are vary widely depending on how they are measured ([Bravata and colleagues, 2020](https://doi.org/10.1007/s11606-019-05364-1)).

Here is the truth:

- **You were accepted onto your programme, or into your role, for a reason.** Someone saw what you are capable of.
- **Everyone has gaps in their knowledge**, including experts.
- **Growth comes from doing the work**, not from doubting your worth.

The fact that you question yourself shows that you care about doing good work. That is a strength, not a weakness. You belong here.

If the feelings become overwhelming, talk to someone you trust: a supervisor, a colleague, or your university’s support services.

## Lesson 21: New ideas often live where fields meet

Don’t limit your reading to your own niche.

Some of my best research ideas came from articles far outside my specific topic.

Reading widely:

- sparks creativity;
- shows you connections others miss;
- helps you approach problems in new ways.

Don’t just read what’s required. Read what’s relevant, wherever it comes from.

## Lesson 22: Curiosity is your most valuable research tool

If there is one trait that separates great researchers from average ones, it is curiosity.

Curiosity is what pushes you to ask one more question, read one more page, test one more variable. Without it, research feels like drudgery. With it, research feels worthwhile.

Many of my best insights didn’t come from structured plans. They came from following my curiosity: a stray footnote, an odd statistic, a “what if” that others might have dismissed.

Train your curiosity:

- **Ask “why?” several times** before you accept an explanation.
- **Don’t dismiss side questions.** Write them down in a notebook, so they don’t distract you now but aren’t lost.
- **Treat anomalies as opportunities**, not inconveniences. The result that doesn’t fit is often the most interesting one.

Curiosity is more than a personality trait. It is a research strategy.

## Lesson 23: Consistency beats intensity

Research breakthroughs rarely come from bursts of genius. They come from steady, consistent effort.

When I started, I worked in erratic sprints: a week of 10-hour days followed by burnout. The result was exhaustion, frustration and stalled progress.

I’ve since learned that showing up consistently, even for two focused hours a day, adds up to a great deal over time.

Some tips:

- **Block out small, regular slots** for research, and protect them.
- **Use a timer to protect your focus.** The Pomodoro technique, working in short timed blocks with breaks in between, works well for many people.
- **Celebrate streaks**, not just milestones.

Consistency is how drafts become dissertations, and dissertations become publications.

## Lesson 26: Build your academic network

For years, I thought networking was for entrepreneurs, not researchers. I believed research was a solo effort.

I was wrong.

Networking in academia isn’t about schmoozing. It is about building relationships that make your work stronger. A conversation with another researcher can lead to:

- a perspective you hadn’t considered;
- a collaboration that strengthens your paper;
- access to data, conferences or future opportunities.

To build your network:

- **Go to conferences to connect**, not just to listen.
- **Get in touch with authors whose work inspired you.** Many researchers are glad to hear their work was useful, and some will reply.
- **Join academic communities online** and take part in the discussion.

Your network is both a safety net and a source of growth.

## Lesson 27: Presenting your work is as important as writing it

A well-written paper is powerful. A well-presented one is memorable.

I learned this the first time I presented at a conference. The feedback, the questions and the opportunities that came from presenting my research went far beyond what the written version achieved on its own.

Presenting forces you to:

- make your argument clear to people outside your own head;
- anticipate hard questions;
- explain your research in a way that works for different people.

Three tips:

- **Practise with non-experts.** If they understand it, you’ve got it right.
- **Use visuals, not walls of text.**
- **Time yourself.** Few things undermine a talk like running over.

Good writing informs. A good presentation can inspire.

## Lesson 30: Celebrate along the way

Research can feel like an endless climb.

It is easy to focus only on the big wins: publishing a paper, finishing your thesis, passing your viva.

But if you don’t celebrate the small wins, you may burn out before you reach the big ones.

When a paper of mine was published in 2025, I had already given myself a pat on the back for every milestone that led up to it. You should too.

- Finished a tough chapter? Celebrate.
- Mastered a new research tool? Celebrate.
- Got constructive feedback instead of a rejection? Celebrate.

> **Pro tip:** keep a “research wins” journal. Writing down every small achievement gives you a record to look back on when doubt creeps in.

Celebrating small wins keeps you motivated for the long road ahead.

## What ties these together

Methods and skills matter, but so does the mindset you bring to them. Accept uncertainty, welcome feedback, remember that you belong, read widely, stay curious, work steadily, connect with others, share your work aloud and celebrate your progress. These are the habits that keep researchers going long enough for the good work to happen.
