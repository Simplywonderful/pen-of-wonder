After months or years of work, you finally have findings to share. But how do you share them in a way that makes people outside your field understand, care and perhaps act?

Research communication often feels rigid: dense papers, long presentations and talks full of jargon. Yet how you communicate your research decides whether anyone beyond a handful of specialists ever uses it.

Research communication means conveying the significance, implications and value of your work to different audiences, from fellow academics to policymakers, practitioners and the general public. This guide covers how to engage those audiences, the mistakes that lose them, and some techniques that go beyond “keep it simple”. The examples are illustrations.

## How to engage a non-specialist audience

### Treat your research as a story

Every study has the shape of a story: a beginning (the problem and your question), a middle (what you did and what you found) and an end (what it means). The order isn’t the hard part. The hard part is making your audience want to know what happens next.

**Example:** in a talk about climate change research, instead of opening with statistics, you could open with a community that is already feeling the effects, then show how your findings help explain what is happening to them. The data becomes about people, and the audience has a reason to listen.

Keep the story honest. Choose examples that represent your findings fairly, and make sure the story never claims more than your data shows.

### Simplify without dumbing down

The central challenge is balancing accuracy with accessibility. Many researchers fall back on jargon and complex language because it feels credible, but it shuts out everyone outside the field.

The goal is to simplify the message without losing what matters. Analogies and familiar examples help.

**Example:** if you need to explain a complex statistical model, you might compare it to a recipe: just as the ingredients and the method shape the final dish, the variables and the techniques shape the result.

### Use the right visuals

Infographics, charts and diagrams can turn a mass of information into something people take in at a glance. But it isn’t about adding visuals; it is about adding the right ones, accurate and chosen to help understanding.

**Example:** in presenting a public health study, a clear chart showing how a behaviour relates to a health outcome will usually reach a general audience better than a table of numbers. Label it plainly, and don’t let the design suggest a stronger relationship than the data shows.

### Make it a conversation

Communication works both ways. Invite your audience in: ask questions, take questions, or run a quick poll in a live session.

**Example:** after presenting your findings, ask, “How do you think these findings could apply in your community?” It makes the session interactive, and it gets people thinking about what your research means in practice.

### Choose your channels, and adapt to each

Research communication isn’t confined to journals and conferences. Social media, blogs, podcasts, webinars, policy briefs and community meetings all reach different people. Each has its own audience and style, so adapt your message to the channel.

**Example:** a short social media post might carry one clear finding and a link to the paper. A blog post or article can explain the background and implications in a more conversational tone. A policy brief needs the recommendation first.

## Research communication mistakes to avoid

### Ignoring your audience’s perspective

A common error is talking about what interests you rather than what concerns your audience. If you are presenting climate research to a general audience, don’t dwell on the details of your models. Show what the findings mean for everyday life. Shape the message around your audience’s needs, concerns and existing knowledge.

### Listing results instead of telling a story

Without a narrative, findings feel dry and disconnected. Instead of listing results, show the problem, how you approached it and why the findings matter, so your audience can see the bigger picture.

### Relying on text alone

Long blocks of text overwhelm people, especially when the content is full of data. A well-chosen chart, diagram or infographic can make a complex point clear and memorable.

### Being too formal

Some formality is right, but stiff, impersonal language puts distance between you and your audience. A conversational tone, where it suits the setting, makes you easier to follow. That doesn’t mean being casual; it means talking to people rather than at them.

### Not inviting a response

One-way communication limits your impact. Don’t end a presentation without inviting questions or comments. Hearing your audience’s reactions shows you what they understood, and what they found important.

## Techniques that go further

### Start with the problem

Instead of opening with your methods or results, start with a real-world problem, and present your findings as part of the answer.

**Example:** if you are discussing the economic effects of a policy, start by showing the difficulties the policy created for the people affected. Your audience will care about the findings because they already care about the problem.

### Use analogies

Analogies link unfamiliar ideas to familiar ones.

**Example:** if your research is about networks, you could compare a network to a road system: the roads are the connections, the cars are the data, and traffic jams are what happens when the system is overloaded. Check that your analogy doesn’t mislead where it matters, and say where it breaks down if that’s important.

### Engage feeling as well as thought

Rigour matters most, but people engage more when a topic connects with their own experience.

**Example:** when talking about the psychological effects of social media, ask your audience to remember a time they felt restless after scrolling through their feed. The research suddenly has something to attach to.

### Treat your audience as collaborators

Ask open questions that make people think about how your findings apply to them. In writing, you can end with a question for the reader, such as “How might this apply in your work?”, or point to where they can follow the work further.

### Explain why before how

Researchers often launch into how they did the work, how the data were collected and analysed, before explaining why it matters. The why is what makes your audience care.

**Example:** if you have developed a new algorithm, first explain the problem with the methods people use now, for instance that they can introduce bias into AI systems, and only then how your approach works.

### Break it into manageable pieces

Present one idea at a time, in short sections or slides, so people can follow each step before the next one arrives. Visual metaphors, a simple image standing for a complex idea, can help each piece stick.

For plain-writing habits that carry over to any audience, see [clear, concise research writing](/blog/clear-concise-research-writing).

## In short

Communicating research well goes beyond simplifying your language. Tell an honest story, start with the problem and the why, use analogies and visuals that clarify rather than decorate, and treat your audience as part of the conversation. Make your work clear enough that people outside your field can understand it, and relevant enough that they want to.
