Starting a literature review can feel like setting sail on a very large sea. Every wave is another paper, and it is easy to lose your bearings. The review is also what gives your study its direction, so it pays to approach it with a method.

This guide covers the process from first search to final write-up: how to find and select sources, how to synthesise and analyse them, the mistakes to watch for at each stage, and how to write the review so it moves your field’s conversation forward. If you are still deciding what kind of review you need, start with my guide to the [types of literature review](/blog/types-of-literature-review).

## Part 1: The process

### 1. Define your research question

Be clear about your question before you read anything. Set out the aims and boundaries of your study, so you can tell what is relevant and what isn’t.

**Example:** if your question is about how well mindfulness-based therapies reduce anxiety, write down the main concepts and variables: which therapies, which population, which measures of anxiety.

### 2. Plan your search

Work out a search strategy before you start, and search more than one place: journal databases, books and grey literature. Use keywords, synonyms, Boolean operators (AND, OR, NOT) and truncation (for example, `educat*` to find education, educational and educator). Look beyond the standard databases too, at repositories and other sources that may hold relevant work.

Keep a record of where you searched, when, and with which terms. You will need it when you write up your method, and it lets you run the same search again later.

### 3. Assess and select your sources

Decide carefully which sources earn a place. Consider the study design, the methods, the sample size, the credibility of the publication and how closely each one relates to your question.

Use an established appraisal tool where one exists. The [CASP checklists](https://casp-uk.net/casp-tools-checklists/), for example, help you appraise systematic reviews, trials, qualitative studies and other designs. Keep appraisal tools separate from reporting guidelines: [PRISMA 2020](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj.n71) is a guideline for *reporting* a systematic review, not a tool for judging the quality of the studies inside it.

### 4. Synthesise and analyse

Look across your sources for recurring themes, trends and gaps. Depending on your question, you might organise the review by theme, by period or by method. Weigh the strengths and weaknesses of earlier research, and draw out what they mean for your question and where more work is needed. Part 2 goes into this in more detail.

### 5. Reflect critically

Engage with the literature rather than simply reporting it. Question assumptions, challenge accepted views where the evidence allows, and point out where the field disagrees. Think about the wider theories that shape your question, and about what current research means for theory, practice and policy.

A thorough review is not a collection of what is known. It is active engagement with ideas, evidence and points of view.

## Part 2: Synthesis and analysis

Synthesis is where a review earns its value. It is also the part most often done badly.

### Synthesising

Synthesis means combining findings from different studies into one coherent account. You assess each study, then look for the patterns, themes and relationships across them.

**Example:** bringing together studies of mindfulness-based therapies for anxiety, you might find the same outcomes appearing again and again: better emotional regulation, lower stress and improved wellbeing.

### Finding themes and patterns

Read past each study’s headline conclusion. Look for the ideas, theoretical frameworks, methods and kinds of evidence that recur across studies.

**Example:** in research on social media and mental health, you might find that social comparison, cyberbullying and online self-presentation come up again and again as factors linked to wellbeing.

### Putting findings in context

Place each finding in its theoretical, methodological and disciplinary setting. Different disciplines and paradigms can read the same evidence differently.

**Example:** studies of sustainability in urban planning look different through the lenses of ecology, sociology, economics and policy studies. Your synthesis should show how each one adds to the picture.

### Analysing critically

Weigh each study’s strengths and weaknesses. Question its assumptions, judge whether its methods are reliable and valid, and consider how far its findings apply to your own question.

**Example:** when you interpret studies of an educational intervention, look at the sample size, the design and any confounding variables that could explain the results.

## Part 3: Mistakes to avoid

### In the search

**Search terms that are too narrow.** Very tight search terms make you miss relevant work. Use synonyms and related terms. If you are studying how technology affects education, search across different technologies, settings and levels before you narrow down.

**Ignoring grey literature.** Reports, theses, dissertations and conference papers often hold findings that never reach the standard databases. Including them makes your review more complete, though they still need the same careful appraisal as anything else.

**Confirmation bias.** Don’t pick out or reinterpret evidence to suit what you already believe, and don’t pass over findings that contradict you. Read with an open mind and a critical eye, and acknowledge the limitations and uncertainties in the research.

**Skipping quality assessment.** Including studies without judging their quality weakens the whole review. Assess each study’s methods, credibility and relevance, using an established tool where you can.

**Missing new work.** A review that stops at the first search goes out of date. Keep up with new publications, and update your review as new evidence appears. Search alerts help, and preprint servers and academic networks can show you work before it is formally published. Treat preprints with care: they have not yet been peer-reviewed.

### In the synthesis

**Trying to read everything.** It is tempting to think more is always better, but trying to absorb everything leads to exhaustion. Read selectively, and concentrate on the work that bears directly on your question.

**Example:** if you are studying how remote work affects productivity, recent studies of the post-pandemic workplace may tell you more than older studies written before today’s tools existed.

**Skimming the methods.** It is easy to rush past the methods section to get to the results, but the detail is where you find out how far to trust them. Check the sample, the statistical methods and how the data were collected. A study with a small, homogeneous sample that claims a dramatic finding may not apply as widely as it suggests.

**Losing the forest for the trees.** You can get so absorbed in individual studies that you miss the larger pattern. A synthesis matrix helps: a table with one row per study and columns for the things you are comparing, such as setting, sample, method and main finding. It shows at a glance where studies agree, where they differ, and where nobody has looked. If several studies agree on one point but disagree on another, that disagreement may be worth researching.

**Ignoring context.** Every study has a setting: a time, a place, a culture. Don’t assume findings travel. Research on teaching methods in Scandinavian schools may not transfer directly to a school system with a different structure and different challenges.

**Leaning on secondary sources.** Summaries and reviews are a good place to start, but they can simplify or misrepresent the original research. When it matters, go back to the primary source and judge the evidence yourself.

Good synthesis balances openness with scepticism, detail with the big picture, and breadth with depth.

## Part 4: Writing the review

### Start with a clear objective

Say what the review is for before you start writing it: its goal, its scope and the research question it serves. If you are studying how social media affects teenagers’ mental health, keep the review focused on that.

### Give it a clear structure

Organise the review in the way that best fits your question: by theme, by period, by theoretical framework or by method.

**Example:** a review of how marketing changed in the digital era might be organised chronologically, to show how the key ideas developed over time.

### Include a range of perspectives

Engage with different views, theories and approaches, and avoid favouring particular authors or schools of thought. A balanced review is more credible, and more useful to your reader.

### Question the literature

Don’t just summarise. Look for gaps, contradictions and new angles, challenge assumptions, and consider what the findings mean for your question.

**Example:** in research on sustainable development, you might question the assumptions and methods behind the sustainability indices that many studies rely on.

### Synthesise your key findings

Draw the threads together. Connect findings, theories and evidence into one account of what is known, and use it to build a framework, raise new questions or propose hypotheses for your own study.

## In short

A good literature review starts with a clear question, rests on a careful and recorded search, judges every source on its merits, and brings the evidence together into an argument of its own. It is more than a summary of earlier research. It is the account that shows why your study needed doing.
