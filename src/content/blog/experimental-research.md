Experiments are the foundation of much scientific inquiry. They let you test hypotheses, identify cause-and-effect relationships and build knowledge in your field. But an experiment is only as good as its design, and its conclusions are only as good as the analysis behind them.

This guide covers what experimental research is, the key decisions to make before you start, and the practices that make an experiment’s results trustworthy. The examples are illustrations, not real studies.

## The basics

### Experimental research

Experimental research investigates cause-and-effect relationships. You change one or more **independent variables**, keep other influences as constant as you can, and measure the effect on one or more **dependent variables**. Because you control what changes, a well-designed experiment lets you draw conclusions about causes, which observational studies usually can’t.

**Example:** a psychologist studying how caffeine affects cognitive performance might give participants different doses of caffeine (the independent variable) and measure their scores on cognitive tests (the dependent variable), in a controlled setting.

### Data analysis

Data analysis means examining the data you have collected to answer your research question or test your hypothesis. In experimental research, it is how you judge whether the effects you observed are real, how large they are, and how confident you can be in them.

The usual tools are descriptive statistics (such as means and standard deviations), inferential statistics (such as t-tests and analysis of variance) and clear graphs.

**Example:** in the caffeine study, you might calculate the mean test score for each caffeine condition, test whether the differences between conditions are statistically significant, and report how big they are.

## Part 1: Key considerations

### Design and hypotheses

Rigorous experimental procedures and precise, testable hypotheses are the cornerstones of experimental research. Define your research question, identify your variables, and state hypotheses that an experiment can actually test, before you collect any data.

**Example:** “Participants who receive 200 mg of caffeine will score higher on a working memory test than participants who receive a placebo.”

### Control and randomisation

Control and randomisation reduce bias, so that any difference you find is more likely to be caused by your manipulation. Keep other variables constant where you can. Then **randomly assign** participants to conditions, so that differences between people, including ones you haven’t measured, are spread by chance across the groups rather than piling up in one of them.

**Example:** in a clinical trial of a new drug, participants would be randomly assigned to receive either the drug or a placebo.

### Sample size and power

Your study needs enough participants to have a good chance of detecting the effect you are looking for, if it exists. A **power analysis**, done before you start, estimates the sample size you need, based on the smallest effect you care about detecting, the significance level you will use (often 0.05) and the power you want (often 80% or 90%). Allow extra for participants who drop out.

**Example:** a study looking for a small effect needs many more participants than one looking for a large effect. A study that is too small may miss a real effect, and if it does find one, it may overestimate its size.

### Ethics and informed consent

When your experiment involves people, ethics comes first. Participants must give informed consent, understanding the purpose of the research, what it involves, its possible risks and benefits, and their right to withdraw. Protect their confidentiality, privacy and wellbeing throughout, and get ethics approval before you begin.

**Example:** studies involving children, or adults who may not be able to consent for themselves, need extra safeguards, such as consent from a parent or legal representative as well as the participant’s own agreement where possible.

### Collecting and analysing data

Use measures and instruments that are reliable and valid for what you want to measure, and apply them the same way to every participant. Decide your main analysis in advance, then use statistical methods that suit your design and your data.

**Example:** to compare learning outcomes between two teaching methods, you might use a t-test; with three or more methods, analysis of variance (ANOVA).

## Part 2: Good practice

### Pilot your procedures

Run a small-scale version of your experiment before the real thing. A pilot shows whether your procedures are feasible, reveals unclear instructions and logistical problems, and lets you fix them before they affect your main data.

**Example:** in a study of a new teaching method, a pilot might show that the instructions to teachers are ambiguous, or that the lesson takes longer than the time available.

### Randomise and counterbalance

Random assignment strengthens your study’s internal validity, its ability to show that the manipulation caused the effect, by reducing the influence of confounding variables.

When the same participants experience more than one condition, the order can matter: people improve with practice, or tire. **Counterbalancing** varies the order systematically to control for these order effects.

**Example:** in a study comparing two memory techniques, half the participants would use technique A first and half would use technique B first, with the order assigned at random.

### Blind where you can

Blinding, or masking, reduces bias from expectations. In a **single-blind** study, participants don’t know which condition they are in. In a **double-blind** study, neither the participants nor the researchers giving the treatment know. Where possible, the people assessing the outcomes should be blinded too.

**Example:** in a drug trial, neither the participants nor the clinicians might know who is receiving the active drug and who the placebo, until the data are analysed.

### Be transparent, and support replication

Replication, repeating a study to see whether its results hold, is how science checks itself. A single experiment, however well done, is one piece of evidence. Results that hold up across different samples, settings and research teams are far more convincing.

Make replication possible by reporting your methods, procedures, analysis and results completely and honestly, including the results that didn’t support your hypothesis. Consider pre-registering your hypotheses and analysis plan. If you are reporting a randomised trial, follow the reporting guideline your journal expects, usually [CONSORT 2025](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2024-081123).

## In short

A good experiment starts with a clear, testable hypothesis and a design that controls what it can and randomises the rest. Plan your sample size, handle ethics with care, pilot your procedures, blind where you can, and analyse your data with methods that suit your design. Then report it all openly, so others can check and build on your work.
