Imagine sitting across from a research participant and having a deep, honest conversation. They tell you things you hadn’t thought to ask about: their ideas, feelings and experiences, in their own words. That is what makes interviews such a useful research method. They give you access to the complexity of people’s behaviour, beliefs and lived experience.

This guide covers what interviews do well, what to consider before you start, and the mistakes to avoid. The examples are illustrations, not real studies.

## The basics

**Qualitative data collection** gathers non-numerical data to understand the depth and richness of human experience. It focuses on people’s experiences, the meanings they give them and how they interpret them.

**Interviews** collect qualitative data through direct conversation with participants, whether tightly structured or more open. They let you explore a subject in depth and hear the detail of people’s accounts.

## Part 1: What interviews do well

### Depth and richness

Interviews give you a detailed understanding of people’s thoughts, emotions and experiences, and the context around them. A questionnaire can tell you how many people feel a certain way; an interview can tell you how and why.

**Example:** in a study of healthcare workers during the COVID-19 pandemic, interviews could capture the emotional toll of the work, how staff coped and the problems they faced in providing care in extraordinary circumstances.

### Flexibility

Interviews adapt to different settings and different participants. You can follow up on what each person says with probing questions, and you can hold interviews in person, by phone or by video call, depending on what suits the participant.

**Example:** in a study of how technology affects older people’s social connections, some participants might prefer a phone call, while others would be more at ease in person.

### Rapport and trust

Participants may share private or sensitive information in interviews, so building trust matters. Empathy, active listening and clear communication help create a non-judgemental atmosphere in which people feel able to speak openly. That openness is what makes the data worth having.

### Ethics and confidentiality

Interviews with people carry ethical responsibilities throughout. You need informed consent, you must protect participants’ confidentiality, and you must respect their autonomy, privacy and dignity.

**Example:** in a study with trauma survivors, you would need to take particular care to avoid causing distress, to let participants pause or stop at any time, and to have support information ready to share.

## Part 2: What to settle before you start

### Your goals and design

Define your research objectives and design before you start interviewing. Be clear about exactly what information you want from the interviews and how it serves your wider research question.

**Example:** if your study explores frontline healthcare workers’ experiences during the pandemic, your questions should be designed to draw out their difficulties, how they coped and what support they needed.

### Who you interview, and how you recruit them

Choose participants who can speak to your research question, and think about access, diversity and relevant experience. In qualitative research you are usually not aiming for statistical representativeness, but for a range of perspectives that covers what you need to understand.

**Example:** if your research focuses on the experiences of minority communities, make deliberate efforts to recruit people from a range of backgrounds within those communities, so that one group’s view isn’t taken to stand for everyone’s.

### Your skills as an interviewer

Good interviewing takes empathy, active listening and interpersonal skill. If you haven’t interviewed before, practise, ask for training, and learn from experienced colleagues. Be aware of your own assumptions, so you can keep them from steering the conversation.

### The structure of the interview

There are three main formats, each with its own strengths:

- **Structured interviews** use fixed questions, often with set response options, so every participant is asked the same thing in the same way. Answers are easy to compare, but there is little room to explore.
- **Semi-structured interviews** use an interview guide of key questions, but let you follow up on what participants say and explore themes as they come up. This is the most common format in qualitative research.
- **Unstructured interviews** are open and conversational, guided by a few broad topics. They allow deep exploration, but are harder to compare and analyse.

Choose the format that fits your research goals and your participants.

### Ethics and informed consent

Before the interview, participants should know the aims of the research, what taking part involves, any risks and benefits, how their data will be stored and used, and that they can withdraw. Get their consent, including consent to record if you plan to, and get ethics approval before you start. Plan how you will respond if a conversation touches on something distressing.

## Part 3: Mistakes to avoid

### Skipping the pilot

Not piloting your interview guide is a common mistake. A pilot interview or two shows up unclear questions, hidden bias and practical problems, before they affect your real data.

**Example:** if you are interviewing first-generation university students about their experiences, a pilot can show whether your questions are relevant, understandable and culturally sensitive, and spare your participants confusion or discomfort.

### Neglecting comfort and trust

If participants don’t feel comfortable, their answers will be guarded or vague, and you lose the depth that makes interviews worth doing.

**Example:** when interviewing trauma survivors, approach each conversation with empathy and sensitivity, so that participants can talk about their experiences without fear of being judged or re-traumatised.

### Ignoring non-verbal cues

What people say is only part of an interview. Body language, tone of voice and facial expressions add context and emotion. Miss them, and you may miss the moment to probe deeper or check what someone meant.

**Example:** if a participant hesitates or seems uneasy on a particular subject, there may be a concern or reservation worth gently exploring, or a boundary worth respecting.

### Asking leading questions

Questions that suggest the answer you expect distort your data.

**Example:** “Don’t you agree that technology has made life more stressful?” pushes the participant towards agreeing. “How, if at all, has technology affected your stress levels?” leaves the answer open.

### Not reflecting on your positionality

Your own background, views and experiences, your positionality, shape how you run interviews and how you interpret what you hear. Reflecting on it throughout the research reduces the risk of misunderstanding or misrepresenting participants.

**Example:** before interviewing people about LGBTQ+ identity, consider how your own identity and assumptions might affect what participants are willing to tell you and how you read their answers.

## In short

Interviews give you depth that few other methods can match. Plan them carefully: be clear about your goals, choose participants thoughtfully, pick the right format and handle consent with care. Then pilot your guide, build trust, listen for what isn’t said, ask open questions and reflect on your own position. Done well, interviews let people’s experiences come through in their own words.
