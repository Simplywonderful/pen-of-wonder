Picture yourself at your desk, surrounded by notes and data printouts. The research is done, and now you have to turn it into a paper. Organising that paper can feel as hard as the research itself.

Plenty of guides cover the basics of introduction, methods, results and conclusion. This one looks at the finer detail that makes each section do its job. It starts with the shape of the whole paper, then works through the first three sections: the introduction, the literature review and the methodology.

A note on the examples: the quoted passages are written as they might appear in a paper. The studies, authors and figures in them are invented for illustration. They are not real research to cite.

## What a research paper is, and how it fits together

A research paper presents your argument, analysis or findings on a question. It brings together evidence, analyses it and sets out the results in a logical order.

Structuring a paper means arranging your ideas, data and conclusions so that a reader can follow them easily. Your target journal’s author guidelines come first, but most empirical papers share the same basic shape.

### Title and abstract

Readers see these first, and many read nothing else. The title should say what the study is about, specifically. The abstract summarises the question, the methods, the main findings and why they matter.

**Example:** instead of a general title such as “Effects of social media”, try “Social media use and academic performance among university students: a quantitative study”.

### Introduction

The introduction sets out your research question, gives the context and shows why the study matters. This is where you win the reader’s attention.

### Literature review

Here you place your study within what is already known. It should be an analytical synthesis that shows the gap your study addresses, not a list of summaries. You might, for instance, set out the conflicting evidence: some studies linking social media use to lower grades, others finding that collaborative tools help learning. Then show what your study does differently.

### Methodology

Describe your design, data collection and analysis, precisely and completely. This is the section that lets readers judge whether your findings can be trusted.

> “Using stratified sampling, we surveyed 500 university students across faculties. We used linear regression in SPSS to examine the association between daily hours of social media use and grade point average.”

### Results

Present your findings clearly and in a logical order, using tables and figures where they help. Report them here; save interpretation for the discussion.

> “Table 1 shows the correlation between daily hours on social media and GPA. The two were moderately and negatively correlated (r = −0.45, p < 0.01).”

### Discussion

Interpret your results and relate them to earlier research and to their wider implications. Address anything unexpected, and offer possible explanations.

> “Our results are consistent with studies reporting an association between heavier social media use and lower grades. They also suggest the association is stronger among first-year students, which may point to where support is most needed.”

Notice the wording: a correlation in a cross-sectional survey shows an association, not that one thing caused the other. The discussion is where that distinction matters most.

### Conclusion

Summarise your main findings, say what they mean, and suggest what future research should look at.

> “These findings suggest that first-year students may benefit most from guidance on managing social media use. Future research should follow students over time and compare specific platforms.”

### Other sections

Most papers also need acknowledgements and references, and some need appendices, a funding statement, a declaration of interests or a data availability statement. Check what your journal asks for.

## The introduction

The introduction is more than a formality. It draws the reader in, sets the scene and states the purpose and significance of your research. Unlike the more technical sections, it is where you tell the story of why your study matters and what gap it fills.

A strong introduction has four parts.

### 1. An opening that draws the reader in

Like the opening scene of a film, your first lines should catch the reader’s interest. Avoid starting with sweeping generalisations. Try a striking fact, a sharp question or a short scenario related to your research, and check any fact you open with against its source.

> “Imagine cancer treatment matched to the genetic make-up of each patient, improving their chances of recovery. That is the promise of personalised medicine. Realising it depends on understanding how genes and disease interact, which is what this study sets out to explore.”

### 2. Background and context

Next, give the reader the background they need to understand your study and its wider setting. Keep it brief: this is a short run-up to your question, not the full literature review.

> “Cancer is a leading cause of death worldwide. Many treatments still follow a one-size-fits-all approach, which does not work equally well for every patient. Recent studies point to the potential of genomics for personalising treatment, but much remains unknown about which genetic markers influence treatment outcomes.”

The first sentence of that example is the kind of claim a reviewer will want referenced. The World Health Organization’s [cancer fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/cancer) is one source for it; cite the current figures when you write.

### 3. The research gap

Show clearly what is missing from current knowledge, and why it matters. This is what tells the reader your study adds something.

> “Many studies have examined the genetic basis of cancer, but few have examined how these genetic factors interact with different treatments. This study addresses that gap by analysing genetic markers in patients receiving different treatments.”

### 4. Aim and objectives

Finally, state the aim of the study and its specific objectives or hypotheses, so the reader knows exactly what to expect.

> “The aim of this study is to identify genetic markers that predict how patients respond to different cancer treatments. It focuses on patients with breast cancer and analyses their genetic profiles in relation to their treatment outcomes.”

The introduction is your first impression. Draw the reader in, give the background, show the gap and state your aims, and the rest of the paper has firm ground to stand on.

## The literature review

The literature review shows your reader how your question fits into the wider field. It is your chance to show that you are adding something new, not repeating what others have said.

### Go beyond summary

The most common weakness in a literature section is that it only summarises earlier studies. Three things take it further.

**Critical analysis.** Don’t just report what others found. Evaluate their methods, results and conclusions, and say how your work relates to them.

> “Author A and colleagues (2018) found a strong correlation between social media use and anxiety in teenagers, but their reliance on self-reported data may have introduced bias. Author B (2019) combined surveys with interviews, which gave a more detailed picture of the relationship, though with a small sample. Research with a larger and more varied sample is still needed.”

**Identifying gaps.** A good review shows what hasn’t been done as well as what has. That is the ground your study stands on.

> “Despite extensive research on the psychological effects of social media, few studies have examined younger children. This study focuses on pre-adolescents.”

**Synthesis.** Show how the studies relate to each other and to yours, and combine them into one argument.

> “Taken together, these studies suggest that the relationship between social media use and mental health depends on factors such as age, gender and socioeconomic status. This study examines how these factors interact in pre-adolescents.”

### Organise it so the argument is easy to follow

A clear structure helps your reader and strengthens your case. Three common approaches:

**By theme.** Group studies by topic rather than by date, so you can discuss patterns across the literature.

> “Research on social media and mental health falls into three main themes: effects on self-esteem, associations with anxiety and depression, and the influence of peer interaction.”

**By method.** Arrange the literature by the methods studies used. This can show how different approaches have produced different findings, and where your study fits.

> “Quantitative studies have mostly examined statistical associations between social media use and mental health outcomes, while qualitative studies have explored people’s experiences in more depth.”

**Detailed analysis of key studies.** Examine a small number of central studies in depth and show how your work builds on them.

> “Author C (2020) followed adolescents for five years, assessing self-esteem twice a year. Its long follow-up shows how self-esteem and social media use change together over time, though, as an observational study, it cannot establish cause. This study builds on that work with a cross-sectional comparison of different age groups.”

You can combine these approaches: themes for the overall structure, with detailed analysis of the key studies within each theme.

## The methodology

The methodology section describes the procedures, methods and tools you used to collect and analyse your data. It should give enough detail for another researcher to repeat the study, and for readers to judge how far the findings can be trusted. Five things make it strong.

### 1. Describe your methods in full

Explain exactly what you did, and why. This shows the thinking behind the study as well as the steps.

> “To examine the relationship between social media use and adolescent self-esteem, we used a mixed methods design. Self-esteem was measured with a Likert-scale questionnaire. Semi-structured interviews explored participants’ experiences in more depth.”

### 2. Justify your choices

Explain why these methods suit your aims and questions.

> “We chose a mixed methods design because neither surveys nor interviews alone could answer both research questions. The survey measured self-esteem across a large sample; the interviews helped explain the patterns it showed.”

### 3. Acknowledge the limitations

Every method has limitations. Say what yours are and what you did to reduce them. This is a sign of honesty, not weakness, and reviewers look for it.

> “Self-reported data may be affected by social desirability bias. To reduce this, responses were anonymous and participants were assured of confidentiality. We also compared the interview findings with the survey results.”

### 4. Ethics

Say how the study was approved and how participants were protected. It is easy to treat this as an afterthought, but journals expect it, and many will not review a paper without it.

> “The study was approved by the university’s research ethics committee. Participants were told the purpose of the study and their right to withdraw at any time, and gave written informed consent. Data were anonymised and stored securely.”

### 5. Data analysis

Describe the analysis step by step: the statistical tests, the software and the criteria you used to interpret the results.

> “Quantitative data were analysed in SPSS, using descriptive statistics and multiple regression to examine relationships between variables. Interview transcripts were coded and analysed thematically in NVivo.”

## In short

A clear structure lets your reader follow your argument from question to conclusion. Open with an introduction that draws the reader in and states your aims, build a literature review that analyses rather than lists, and write a methodology complete enough for others to judge and repeat your work.
