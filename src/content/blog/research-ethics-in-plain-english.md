Like many researchers, I have had moments in my work that changed how I saw it. One of them was coming to understand, properly, how much responsible conduct and ethics matter in research.

When I started out, I met ethical questions I didn’t fully understand, and I found myself at crossroads I wasn’t prepared for. Since I know better now, one of the most useful things I can pass on is a clearer picture of what research ethics involves.

This guide covers two things: the parts of research ethics that go beyond the textbook, and the ethical problems that most often catch researchers out.

## What does research ethics involve?

### The people behind the data

Ethics goes beyond checklists, consent forms and paperwork. Every data point from a participant comes from a person with their own history, and your research can affect their life. Keep that in mind at every stage, from how you recruit people to how you write about them.

### Conflicts of interest

Conflicts of interest can be subtle. As well as financial links, there are personal relationships and commitments that can affect your objectivity. Recognise and declare them from the start. If you don’t, readers will reasonably question your findings.

### The wider effects of your work

Think about how your research could affect society, the environment or culture, not just your field. Its consequences may reach well beyond the academic community, and some of them may be ones you didn’t intend.

### Inclusion

Inclusion is more than ticking boxes. It means designing studies in which different perspectives are actively sought and valued, not just tolerated. Where your question calls for it, make sure your sample includes the people whose views are often left out, and that they shape the findings rather than appearing only as numbers.

### New technology

New technologies raise new ethical questions, and the answers aren’t always settled. If you use AI tools, new kinds of data or new ways of collecting it, think through what they mean for privacy, consent, accuracy and fairness, and check your university’s or journal’s current policy before you start.

### Transparency

Transparency is at the core of research integrity. Report your methods, your possible biases and your limitations, as well as your results. Openness builds trust with other researchers and with the public.

### A shared culture

Ethical research is a shared responsibility, not just an individual one. Seminars, open discussion and mentoring all help build a culture in which people raise concerns early and learn from each other’s mistakes.

## Ethical problems that catch researchers out

### Authorship disputes

Authorship disputes are common and painful. Prevent them by agreeing roles, contributions and author order early, and revisiting the agreement as the project changes. Recognise every person’s intellectual contribution. Many journals follow the International Committee of Medical Journal Editors’ criteria, under which an author must contribute substantially to the work, help draft or critically revise it, approve the final version, and agree to be accountable for it. People who contributed but don’t meet all four should be acknowledged instead ([ICMJE](https://www.icmje.org/recommendations/browse/roles-and-responsibilities/defining-the-role-of-authors-and-contributors.html)).

### Predatory journals

Even careful researchers can be drawn in by predatory journals, which take a fee and publish with little or no real peer review. Check a journal before you submit: who publishes it, how its peer review works, what it charges, and where it is indexed. Be wary of impressive-sounding metrics you can’t verify, since some predatory journals advertise invented ones. The [Think. Check. Submit.](https://thinkchecksubmit.org/journals/) checklist is a good place to start.

### Data handling

There is a clear line between collecting and analysing data honestly and manipulating it. Under pressure to produce a striking result, it can be tempting to drop inconvenient cases, try analyses until one “works”, or tidy numbers that don’t fit. Don’t. Fabrication (making up data or results) and falsification (manipulating data or methods to give a false impression) are serious research misconduct. Record what you did, report what you found, and explain any data you excluded and why.

### Consent on sensitive topics

Informed consent matters most when the subject is sensitive. Explain clearly what taking part involves and what its possible effects are, in language participants understand. Make sure people know they can stop or withdraw, and that saying no carries no penalty.

### Conflicts of interest, again

Conflicts of interest threaten objectivity quietly, so review your connections, relationships and financial links regularly, not just once. Declare anything relevant openly to keep your readers’ trust.

### Plagiarism

Plagiarism defeats the purpose of research and destroys its integrity. It isn’t only about copying words. If an idea, a phrase or a contribution isn’t yours, credit the person it came from. Paraphrasing someone’s work without citing them is still plagiarism.

### People left out

Not every voice gets equal weight in research. Where your question calls for it, make your sample as inclusive and diverse as you can, so that minority groups contribute to the story instead of appearing as footnotes.

Plagiarism is covered in more depth in [referencing and plagiarism](/blog/referencing-and-plagiarism), and checking that a journal is genuine in [choosing a journal and getting your manuscript submitted](/blog/choosing-a-journal-and-submitting).

## In short

Research ethics is more than approval forms. It means remembering the people behind your data, being open about conflicts and limitations, thinking about the wider effects of your work, and dealing honestly with authorship, publication, data and other people’s ideas. Unethical decisions can end even the most promising research careers. Careful, honest ones build the trust that good research depends on.
