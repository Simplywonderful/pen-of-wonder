In 2025 I shared thirty lessons from my years as a researcher, one at a time. This piece brings together four of them about writing: how to start, how to be understood, how to improve a draft, and how to keep going until it is finished.

## Lesson 8: Write a strong introduction

Your introduction often decides whether someone reads your work closely or skims past it to something else. Readers have a lot to read, and they decide quickly what deserves their time.

The introduction isn’t just the start of your paper. It is your chance to win your reader’s attention and earn their trust.

A strong introduction:

- frames the problem clearly;
- explains why your study matters;
- tells the reader what to expect from the rest of the paper.

Think of it as an elevator pitch for your research: short, compelling and precise. Your reader should finish it feeling both informed and curious to read on.

> **Pro tip:** many researchers write the introduction last, or at least revise it last. Once you know exactly what your paper found and argues, it is much easier to introduce it well.

## Lesson 9: Clarity beats complexity

The smartest people I know explain complex ideas simply.

Some researchers think long, complicated sentences make their work sound academic. In reality, unclear writing loses the reader and weakens the message.

Clarity is not the same as simplification. It is precision. Even the most sophisticated ideas can usually be put in straightforward language.

Here is my checklist for clarity:

- **Avoid jargon unless you need it**, and define it when you do.
- **Prefer the active voice** where it reads naturally. “We interviewed 20 nurses” is clearer than “Interviews were conducted with 20 nurses.” Some methods sections and journals still prefer the passive, so check your target journal’s style.
- **Keep each sentence to one idea.**

Good research deserves to be understood, not decoded. If your reader understands your work, they can value it. If they don’t, they can’t.

I go into this in more detail, with before-and-after examples, in my guide to [clear, concise research writing](/blog/clear-concise-research-writing).

## Lesson 17: Great writing happens in the edits

The real work of research writing doesn’t happen in the first draft. It happens in the rewrites.

My first drafts are messy, and that’s fine. Good research writing isn’t about getting it perfect the first time. It is about refining it until it is clear, logical and precise.

Editing is where:

- ideas sharpen;
- repetition disappears;
- the argument becomes clear.

If you want your work to be read, spend as much time revising as you do drafting. That is how messy thoughts become clear arguments.

## Lesson 24: Writing is a marathon, so pace yourself

Writing in academia is not a one-day sprint. It is a marathon.

I used to think I had to write the “perfect” paper in one sitting. I never could. What I got instead was procrastination and anxiety.

Real progress came when I started treating writing like training for a marathon:

- **Pace yourself.** Write a little every day, even if it is only 200 words.
- **Build endurance.** Accept that revision is part of the process, not a sign you got it wrong.
- **Expect discomfort.** Some days writing feels heavy. That is normal, and it passes.

Don’t aim for perfection in the first draft. Aim for completion. You can always refine it later.

The marathon mindset keeps you from burning out, and it keeps the work moving.

## What ties these together

Good research writing is built, not produced in one go. Open with an introduction that earns attention, write as plainly as your ideas allow, give revision the time it deserves, and keep a steady pace so you reach the end. The first draft only has to exist. The edits make it good.
