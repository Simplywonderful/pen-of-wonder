A grant proposal for a small NGO has one job: to convince a funder that a real problem exists, that your plan will change it, and that your organisation can be trusted with the money. Start with the funder's own guidance and follow it exactly. Then build your proposal around a clear problem backed by evidence, objectives you can measure, activities and a budget that match each other, and honest proof that you can deliver.

## In short

- The funder's guidance comes first. Use their headings, order and word limits.
- Check you fit the funder's priorities before you write a word.
- Describe the problem with evidence, not adjectives.
- Write objectives someone could check at the end of the project.
- Make sure every budget line links to an activity, and every activity has a cost.

## Before you write: are you a good fit?

The most common reason a proposal fails is that it should never have been sent. Before you start, read the funder's guidelines and check four things:

1. **Priorities.** Does the funder support your kind of work, in your area, for your group of people?
2. **Eligibility.** Is your organisation the right type and size? Do you have the registration and documents they ask for, such as audited accounts?
3. **Amount.** Is the grant size close to what your project needs?
4. **Deadline.** Do you have enough time to write it properly and gather the documents?

If the answer to any of these is no, move on. A strong proposal sent to the wrong funder is still a rejection.

## What sections does a grant proposal have?

Every funder has its own form, and you should always follow it. But most proposals ask for the same core sections, under different names.

| Section | What it answers | Common mistake |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Executive summary | What, who, where, how much, and why it matters | Writing it first, before you know what the proposal says |
| Problem statement | What's wrong, for whom, and how you know | No evidence, or a problem far bigger than the project |
| Objectives | What will be different by the end | Activities written as objectives |
| Activities and timeline | What you'll do, when and who does it | No link between activities and objectives |
| Monitoring and evaluation | How you'll know it worked | Only counting activities, not changes |
| Budget and budget narrative | What it costs and why | Costs that don't match the activities |
| Organisational capacity | Why you can deliver | Vague claims with no track record |

## How do you write the problem statement?

Describe the problem as precisely as you can, for a specific group of people in a specific place, and show how you know. Good evidence includes government statistics, published studies, reports from other organisations, and your own records or surveys.

Then narrow it down. Don't write as if your project will solve youth unemployment across a whole country. Write about the young people in your two local government areas who finish school without the skills local employers are asking for, and what you've seen of it.

## How do you write objectives a funder believes?

An objective describes a change, not an activity. "Train 60 women in bookkeeping" is an activity. "By the end of the project, at least 40 of the 60 women trained are keeping weekly business records" is an objective.

A useful test is the SMART check: is the objective **specific**, **measurable**, **achievable**, **relevant** to the problem, and **time-bound**? If someone visited at the end of the project, could they tell whether you'd met it?

## How do the activities, budget and results fit together?

Funders read for consistency. Every activity should serve an objective. Every activity should have a cost in the budget. Every cost should have an activity. And your monitoring plan should measure the objectives, not just count the activities.

A simple way to check is to make a table for yourself before you write the final version:

| Objective | Activities | Budget lines | How you'll measure it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 40 of 60 women keep weekly records | 6 training sessions; 3 follow-up visits each | Trainer fees; venue; transport; record books | Visit records at months 3 and 6 |

If a row is missing something, the proposal has a gap a reviewer will find.

## How do you show your organisation can deliver?

Show, don't claim. Instead of "We are a dedicated, experienced organisation", give:

- how long you've been working, and where
- one or two projects like this one, with what they achieved
- who will run this project, and their relevant experience
- how you manage money, such as audited accounts or a finance policy

Small organisations sometimes worry they have little to show. Be honest about your size, and point to what you have done well. Funders who support small NGOs expect them to be small.

## A final check before you submit

- [ ] You've used the funder's headings, order and word limits.
- [ ] The executive summary was written last and matches the proposal.
- [ ] The problem statement has evidence, with sources.
- [ ] Each objective describes a change you can measure.
- [ ] Every activity has a cost, and every cost has an activity.
- [ ] The monitoring plan measures objectives, not just activities.
- [ ] All requested documents are attached.
- [ ] Someone outside the project has read it and understood it.
- [ ] You're submitting before the deadline, not on it.
