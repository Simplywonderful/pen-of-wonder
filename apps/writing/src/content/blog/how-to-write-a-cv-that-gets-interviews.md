A CV that gets interviews does one job: it shows a busy reader, in a few seconds, that you can do this particular role. It opens with a short profile written for that job. It shows what you achieved, not just what you were responsible for. It puts the most relevant experience where the eye lands first. And it's plain enough for both a person and a screening system to read without effort.

## In short

- Write for one job at a time. Adjust your CV to each advert.
- Open with a three or four line profile aimed at the role.
- Turn duties into results: what changed because you were there?
- Keep the layout plain, with a clear font at size 11 or bigger.
- Check every line against the job advert. If it doesn't help, cut it.

## What should a CV include?

The UK National Careers Service says a CV should include "your contact details, an introduction, your education history, your work history and references". Most CVs also need a skills section. In practice, a strong CV has six parts:

| Section | What goes in it | Tip |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Contact details | Name, phone, email, town or city, LinkedIn link if it's up to date | No photo, date of birth or full address unless the job asks |
| Profile | Three or four lines on who you are and what you offer this employer | Rewrite it for every job |
| Key skills | Six to ten skills that match the advert | Use the advert's own words where true |
| Work history | Most recent first, with results under each job | Two to five lines per role |
| Education and training | Qualifications, courses and certificates | Most recent first |
| References | "Available on request", or names if the advert asks | Ask your referees first |

## How do you write a profile that gets read?

The profile is the first thing a recruiter reads, so make it about the job, not about you in general. A good profile answers three questions in three or four lines: what you do, what you're good at, and what you want to do for this employer.

**Weak:** "Hard-working, motivated professional seeking a challenging role where I can grow."

**Stronger:** "Customer service team lead with four years in retail banking, managing a team of six and handling complaints from first contact to resolution. Looking to bring that experience to a service manager role in financial services."

The second version is specific, it names the kind of role, and every claim in it can be checked against the rest of the CV.

## How do you turn duties into achievements?

Most CVs list what the person was supposed to do. Strong CVs show what happened because they did it. For each job, ask yourself: what was better when I left than when I started?

| Duty (weak) | Achievement (stronger) |
| --- | --- |
| Responsible for social media | Grew the company's Instagram following from 1,200 to 4,000 in eight months by posting three times a week |
| Handled customer complaints | Resolved around 30 complaints a week, and cut the backlog from two weeks to three days |
| Managed the office budget | Kept a yearly office budget within limits for three years running |

Use numbers where you honestly have them. Where you don't, describe the change in plain words. Never invent a figure; an interviewer will ask about it.

## How do you tailor a CV to each job?

You don't need to rewrite your CV from scratch for every application. Keep one full "master" version with everything in it, then for each job:

1. Read the advert and underline the skills and experience it asks for.
2. Rewrite your profile to match the role.
3. Move your most relevant experience higher, and cut what doesn't help.
4. Use the advert's words for skills you genuinely have. If it says "stakeholder management" and you've been doing exactly that, call it that.

This takes twenty to thirty minutes per application. It's the difference between a CV that could be for anyone and one that's clearly for this job.

## How should a CV look?

Plain beats clever. The National Careers Service suggests a clear font "like Arial, Times New Roman or Calibri in size 11 or bigger". Beyond that:

- Use clear headings and plenty of white space.
- Avoid tables, columns, text boxes and graphics for the main content. They can confuse the applicant tracking systems many employers use to sort applications. I explain why in [How applicant tracking systems read your CV](/blog/how-applicant-tracking-systems-read-your-cv).
- Save it as a PDF unless the advert asks for a Word file, and name the file with your name, such as "Ada-Okafor-CV.pdf".
- Read it out loud before you send it. You'll catch mistakes your eyes skip.

## How long should a CV be?

Long enough to show you can do the job, and no longer. In the UK, two pages is the usual expectation for most experienced candidates. Early-career applicants often fit everything on one. In the US, employers usually expect a shorter résumé, often one page, unless you have many years of experience.

If you're over two pages, cut the oldest and least relevant roles down to one line each, or remove them. Nobody needs the detail of a job from fifteen years ago.

## A last check before you send it

- [ ] Your profile mentions the kind of role you're applying for.
- [ ] Every job has at least one result, not only duties.
- [ ] The skills section uses the advert's words where they're true for you.
- [ ] Contact details are correct, with a professional email address.
- [ ] There are no spelling mistakes. Read it aloud, then ask someone else to read it.
- [ ] The file is a PDF with your name in the file name.
