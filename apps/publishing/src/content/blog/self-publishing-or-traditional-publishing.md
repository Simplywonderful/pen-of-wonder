Traditional publishing costs you nothing up front: the publisher pays for editing, design, printing and distribution, and pays you. In return you give up control, a share of the earnings and a lot of time. Self-publishing gives you control, speed and the rights, but you pay for everything and you do the selling. Neither is better for every book. The right choice depends on what your book is, who it's for, and what you want from it.

This is general guidance, not legal advice. Publishing contracts differ, so get advice on any contract before you sign.

## In short

- **Traditional**: no cost to you, a publisher's team behind the book, wider shop distribution, but slow and selective, with less control.
- **Self-publishing**: fast, full control and rights, but you fund and manage every step, and selling is your job.
- **Hybrid publishers** charge the author and share the earnings. Some are reputable and some aren't. Read the contract with care.
- Paying to be published is never traditional publishing.

## How do the two compare?

| | Traditional publishing | Self-publishing |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Who pays for editing, design and printing | The publisher | You |
| Who gets paid first | You may receive an advance | You earn from each sale |
| Control over title, cover and changes | The publisher usually decides, with your input | You decide |
| Time to publication | Long. Penguin says 18 to 24 months is not unusual for a picture book | As fast as the work allows |
| Getting into bookshops | Easier, through the publisher's sales team | Harder; possible through distributors |
| Who markets the book | Shared, but authors are expected to help | You |
| Rights | Licensed to the publisher under the contract | Yours |
| How hard it is to get in | Very selective | Anyone can |

## When does traditional publishing make sense?

Traditional publishing suits you if:

- you want a publisher's editors, designers and sales team behind your book
- you want it in bookshops widely, from the start
- you're willing to spend time finding an agent or publisher, and accept rejection along the way
- you're comfortable letting the publisher make some decisions about the title, cover and timing

For children's picture books, Penguin notes you don't need to find an illustrator before approaching a publisher; the publisher usually does that.

## When does self-publishing make sense?

Self-publishing suits you if:

- you want the book out within months, not years
- you want to decide on the cover, the title and the price
- you can pay for good editing, design and printing
- you can reach your readers directly: through schools, churches, events, a business or an online following
- the book serves a specific audience that publishers may not see as big enough

That last point matters more than people think. A book written for one community, one profession or one curriculum can do very well self-published, because the author knows exactly where its readers are.

## What about hybrid publishers and vanity presses?

Between the two sits a third option: companies that charge the author to publish the book.

- **Hybrid publishers** usually charge for some or all of the costs, then share sales income. Some are selective and do good work.
- **Vanity presses** publish almost anything, as long as the author pays. Their income comes mainly from authors, not from selling books.

Before you pay any company to publish your book, find out:

1. **Who owns the rights**, and for how long?
2. **What exactly you're paying for**: editing, design, printing, distribution, marketing?
3. **What share of each sale you receive**, and how often you're paid?
4. **Can you leave**, and what happens to your files and rights if you do?
5. **Which of their books sell**, and where?

If the answers are vague, walk away. If a company tells you that paying them is how traditional publishing works, it isn't.

## A quick way to decide

Ask yourself three questions:

1. **Who is the book for, and how will they find it?** If you can reach them directly, self-publishing is a real option.
2. **How soon does it need to exist?** If you need it for a school year, a launch or an event, traditional timelines may not fit.
3. **What do you want most: control, or a publisher's backing?** You can't usually have both.

If you choose to self-publish, [How to self-publish a book, step by step](/blog/how-to-self-publish-a-book-step-by-step) walks through each stage. There's no shame in either route. Many authors use both at different times, for different books.

This is general guidance, not legal advice. Rules and contracts differ by country, so check with a lawyer or an authors' organisation before you sign anything.
