To write a children's picture book, start with one small story for one age group, and keep it short. Penguin says story-based picture books "are usually 32 pages long and vary between 500 to 1000 words". Write the words so the pictures can carry part of the story, plan where each page turns, and read the text aloud until every word has earned its place.

I wrote the six Hidden Treasures Companion Readers for Nigerian primary classes, so this guide comes from making books for children as well as reading about it.

## In short

- Pick one age group and write for them, not for adults.
- Aim for 500 to 1,000 words for a story picture book.
- Let the pictures tell what the words don't need to.
- Plan the page turns: each one should make a child want to see the next.
- Read it aloud, many times, to children if you can.

## Who is the book for?

Decide on your reader first, because it changes everything else: the words you use, the length, and who will be holding the book.

Penguin describes story picture books as "designed for pre-schoolers and children in the first few years of primary school", usually "read aloud as a shared experience between an adult and child". So you are writing for two people at once: the child who listens and looks, and the adult who reads. The words need to sound good out loud, and they need to hold up on the twentieth reading.

| Reader | What they need from the book |
| --- | --- |
| Toddlers and pre-schoolers | Very few words, repetition, things to point at and name |
| Children starting school | A simple story with a clear problem and ending, words they can join in with |
| Children reading alone for the first time | Short sentences, familiar words, pictures that support the text |

If you're writing for children who are learning to read by themselves, you're writing an early reader rather than a picture book, and the rules are stricter. I'll cover that in its own post.

## What makes a good picture book story?

The best picture book stories are small. One character, one problem, one change. A child loses a favourite thing and finds it. A child is scared of something and finds the courage to face it. A child wants something and learns something along the way.

Ask yourself three questions about your idea:

1. **Who is the main character, and what do they want?**
2. **What gets in the way?**
3. **How has the character changed by the last page?**

If you can't answer all three in a sentence each, the story isn't ready yet.

## How do you write so the pictures can do their part?

In a picture book, the words and the pictures share the work. Penguin's editors put it this way: picture book writers "don't need to show everything in the story, but should also not rely on the illustrations to do all of the storytelling work".

In practice, that means:

- **Don't describe what the picture will show.** If the illustration shows a red bicycle, you don't need to write "a shiny red bicycle".
- **Let the pictures add a joke or a second story.** The words say "Everything was quiet". The picture shows a goat eating the washing.
- **Add short illustration notes only where needed.** If the meaning depends on something the words don't say, add a brief note in brackets for the illustrator.

## How do you plan the pages?

A 32-page book doesn't give you 32 pages of story. Some go to the title page, the copyright page and other front matter. So plan your story across roughly 12 to 14 spreads (two facing pages).

A simple way to plan:

1. Fold eight sheets of paper in half and staple them into a little booklet.
2. Mark the first few pages for the title and copyright.
3. Write your text across the remaining spreads, a few lines at a time.
4. Look at each page turn. Does it make the child want to know what happens next?

Page turns are a picture book's secret tool. End a spread on a question, a surprise or a "but then...", and the child will turn the page for you.

## How do you know when the words are right?

Read the whole book aloud. Then read it aloud again. You'll hear the sentences that are too long, the words that trip your tongue, and the parts where a child's attention would drift.

Then, if you can, read it to children of the right age, and watch them rather than asking what they thought. Where do they lean in? Where do they look away? That tells you more than any adult's opinion.

Cut every word you can. In a book of 600 words, each word is a tenth of a percent of the whole.

## What comes after the writing?

Once the text is finished, you have two main routes:

- **Traditional publishing.** You send the text to publishers or agents, usually without illustrations. Penguin notes that the whole process can take "between 18 and 24 months".
- **Self-publishing.** You hire an illustrator, a designer and a printer yourself, and you keep control and the rights. It's faster, and it costs money up front. The steps are in [How to self-publish a book](/blog/how-to-self-publish-a-book-step-by-step).

If you're not sure which route fits, read [Self-publishing or traditional publishing?](/blog/self-publishing-or-traditional-publishing)

Either way, get the words right first. A beautiful illustration can't save a story that doesn't work.
