In 2025 I shared thirty lessons from my years as a researcher, one at a time. This piece brings together four of them about the evidence your research rests on: your data, your methods, the files that hold them, and your ability to make sense of the numbers.

## Lesson 10: Data is gold, so treat it that way

Data is the backbone of your research. Without it, a claim is just an opinion.

In research, your opinions are not what persuades people. Evidence is: findings that come from data collected and analysed properly.

Good data is:

- **accurate**, because it was collected systematically;
- **relevant**, because it links directly to your research question;
- **reliable**, because it can be checked and the process repeated.

Treat your data like the valuable asset it is. Document how you collected it, keep your datasets safe, and check them for errors before you analyse anything.

> **Pro tip:** keep an untouched copy of your raw data, and do all your cleaning and analysis on a working copy. Keep a short log of every change you make. If you ever need to retrace a step, or a reviewer asks how you got from the raw data to your results, you will be able to show them.

In the academic world, data is currency. Protect it and manage it wisely. Good data is what makes results credible.

## Lesson 13: A solid methodology is your map to credible findings

Your methodology is more than a chapter in your dissertation. It is what drives your research.

It shows the route you took, the choices you made and why you made them. It tells your readers exactly how you reached your conclusions, and whether they can trust your results.

A good methodology is:

- **clear**, so that others could repeat it;
- **justified**, so that every choice has a reason;
- **aligned**, so that it fits your research question.

If your methodology is weak, your conclusions will always be questioned. If it is strong, your findings will stand up to scrutiny.

## Lesson 25: Back up your files, and save yourself the heartbreak

Nothing hurts like losing weeks, or months, of research to a crashed laptop or a corrupted file.

I’ve been there. It’s devastating.

Now I treat backups as non-negotiable. My rule is simple: if it isn’t backed up, it doesn’t exist.

Here is what I’d suggest:

- **Keep at least two backups:** one local, such as an external hard drive, and one in the cloud, such as Google Drive, Dropbox or OneDrive.
- **Automate your backups**, so they don’t depend on you remembering.
- **Version your files** with clear names and dates, and avoid the “final_FINAL_v3.docx” nightmare.

> **Pro tip:** a folder that syncs to the cloud is not quite the same as a backup. If you delete or overwrite a file, the change syncs too. Check that your cloud service keeps earlier versions, or keep a separate backup as well. And if your data includes information about participants, check your ethics approval and your university’s data policy before you store it anywhere: some data may only be kept on approved, secure storage.

Backing up your research isn’t optional. It is part of surviving academia.

## Lesson 28: Understanding data makes you a stronger researcher

For the longest time, I told myself: “I’m not a numbers person.”

But here is the truth. In research, some level of data literacy isn’t optional.

Even if your field is mainly qualitative, sooner or later you will meet statistics, survey results or datasets you need to make sense of.

Knowing the basics of data analysis gives you an edge. Not because you will become a statistician, but because you will understand the story behind the numbers.

When I finally learned simple descriptive statistics, how to read regression output, and how to use tools like Excel and SPSS, it didn’t just boost my confidence. It made my research more credible.

Here is where to start:

- **Learn the basics:** averages, standard deviation and correlation.
- **Start with tools you already have**, such as Google Sheets or Excel, before you move on to specialist software.
- **Don’t fear the maths.** Focus on understanding and interpreting the results, not on perfection.

You don’t have to be a quantitative expert. But ignoring data completely limits what your research can do.

## What ties these together

Your conclusions are only as trustworthy as the evidence behind them. Collect and handle your data with care, explain and justify how you worked, protect your files from the day you create them, and learn enough about numbers to read them for yourself. None of it is glamorous, but all of it is what makes your findings worth believing.
