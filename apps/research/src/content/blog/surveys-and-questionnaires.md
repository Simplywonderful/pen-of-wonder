Surveys and questionnaires are among the most common ways to collect data in academic research, especially quantitative data. Used well, they tell you what large numbers of people think, believe and do. Used carelessly, they produce a spreadsheet full of answers nobody can rely on.

This guide covers what these tools do well, what to decide before you design one, the most common mistakes, and practical tips for collecting data you can actually use.

## Surveys and questionnaires: what’s the difference?

The two words are often used interchangeably, but they mean slightly different things.

- **A survey** is the overall method: a systematic way of collecting data from a sample of people by asking them a set of structured questions. It can be carried out face to face, by phone, on paper or online.
- **A questionnaire** is the instrument: the written set of questions itself. It can be completed by respondents on their own or read out by a researcher. Many are now run online, using tools such as Google Forms or Qualtrics.

## Part 1: What they do well

**Flexibility.** You can choose from many formats and ways of reaching people: in person, by phone or online. That lets you adapt to different research settings and to what suits your participants.

**Reach and efficiency.** A survey can reach a large number of people at once, quickly and cheaply, which makes large datasets practical even on a small budget.

**Standardisation.** Everyone answers the same questions with the same response options. That consistency makes it easier to compare groups, or to compare your results with other studies that used the same measures, and makes your findings easier to check and repeat.

**Anonymity and confidentiality.** When people can answer without anyone watching, and know their answers can’t be traced back to them, they tend to be more open about sensitive or personal topics. Research on sensitive questions shows that people often edit their answers to avoid embarrassment, especially in front of an interviewer ([Tourangeau and Yan, 2007](https://doi.org/10.1037/0033-2909.133.5.859)), so the way you collect answers matters.

**Adaptability.** You can shape the questions, wording, language and tone to suit your participants and your aims, and add images or other features where they help people understand.

## Part 2: What to settle before you start

### Your research goals and design

Be clear about your objectives and your design before you write a single question. Your target population, your scope and the results you need all affect what kind of survey you choose and how you lay it out.

### Validity and reliability

Your questionnaire has to measure what it is meant to measure (validity), and do so consistently (reliability). Where you can, use established scales that have already been tested with people like your participants. Pilot your instrument, and where you build your own scale, check its content and construct validity and its reliability with the appropriate statistical tests.

### Ethics

Follow your institution’s ethics process. Get informed consent, protect participants’ privacy and confidentiality, store the data securely, and keep any risk of harm or discomfort to a minimum.

### Your sampling strategy

If you want your results to say something about a wider population, your sample has to represent it. Think carefully about your sampling frame, your sampling method and your sample size before you start, not after.

### Response rate and non-response bias

You want as many people as possible to respond, and you want the people who respond to be like the people who don’t. Those are not the same thing. Several things reliably raise response rates: a large review of randomised trials found that incentives, reminders, contacting people before sending the questionnaire, and shorter questionnaires all increased response to postal questionnaires ([Edwards and colleagues, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1002/14651858.MR000008.pub5)).

A high response rate on its own doesn’t guarantee unbiased results, though. A meta-analysis of studies that measured non-response bias directly found that response rates were only weakly linked to how biased the results were ([Groves and Peytcheva, 2008](https://doi.org/10.1093/poq/nfn011)). What matters is whether the people who didn’t respond differ from those who did, in ways that affect your answers.

## Part 3: Common mistakes

### Complex or leading questions

Complicated or leading questions confuse people and push them towards a particular answer.

**Example:** “Do you agree that the government’s policies are damaging the economy?” invites agreement. A more neutral version asks: “What effect, if any, do you think the government’s economic policies have had on the economy?”, with a balanced set of response options from very positive to very negative.

### Skipping the pilot

If you don’t pilot your questionnaire with a small group first, problems you could easily have fixed reach every respondent: unclear instructions, confusing questions, formatting errors, broken skip logic or technical faults.

### Too few response options

If the answer options are too limited, people can’t express what they actually think.

**Example:** a simple “yes” or “no” to a question about a complex experience oversimplifies the data. A scale, or a “partly” option, may capture it better.

### Ignoring non-response bias

Assume the people who didn’t respond are just like those who did, and you may draw the wrong conclusions about your population. Compare responders and non-responders on whatever you know about both, and report the response rate and its limits honestly.

### Ignoring culture

Questions that don’t account for culture can be misunderstood, or can put off respondents from particular backgrounds. Check that your wording, examples and assumptions make sense to everyone in your sample. If you are translating a questionnaire, translate it carefully and test the translated version too.

## Part 4: Tips for better surveys

### Design for your audience

Think about who your respondents are, and adapt the language, tone and content to suit them. Questions written for academics won’t work for school pupils, and the reverse is also true.

### Use branching and skip logic

Route people past questions that don’t apply to them, based on their earlier answers. It shortens the survey, cuts repetition and keeps people engaged.

### Keep it clear and consistent

Use plain, unambiguous wording, and keep the layout and response options consistent throughout, so respondents read every question the same way.

### Mix closed and open questions

Closed questions give structured answers that are easy to analyse. Open questions, the “what”, “how” and “why” questions, let people explain in their own words. A few well-placed open questions can tell you why the numbers look the way they do.

### Respect people’s time

Keep the survey short and focused, so respondents don’t tire and start answering carelessly. Think about timing too: send it when your respondents are most likely to have time to answer.

### Give clear instructions

Tell respondents what the survey is for, how long it will take and how to complete it. Define any terms that might be unclear.

### Plan the analysis before you collect the data

Decide how you will analyse each question before the survey goes out: statistical analysis for the closed questions, and thematic analysis or coding for the open ones. If you can’t say how you will use a question, you probably don’t need it. For more on the analysis itself, see my guide to [quantitative data analysis](/blog/quantitative-data-analysis).

## In short

A good survey starts with clear goals, a tested instrument, an ethical process and a sampling plan. Write clear, neutral questions, pilot them, give people the answer options they need, and think hard about who isn’t responding. Get these right, and your survey will give you data you can analyse with confidence.
