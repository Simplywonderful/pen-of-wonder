You can rarely study everyone you are interested in. Instead, you study a sample, and use it to say something about the wider group. How you choose that sample decides how far your findings can be trusted, and how far they can be applied beyond the people you studied.

This guide covers the key terms, the most common sampling techniques and how to choose between them.

## What is sampling in research?

- **Population:** the whole group you want to draw conclusions about, such as all nursing students in Nigeria, or all small businesses in one city.
- **Sample:** the people, things or events you actually study, selected from that population.
- **Sampling:** the process of choosing the sample. Done well, it uses a systematic, deliberate method so that the sample fits the purpose of the study.
- **Sampling frame:** the list or source you select from, such as a register of students or a list of registered businesses. Gaps in the frame become gaps in your sample.
- **Sampling technique:** the method you use to choose the sample. Different techniques suit different research settings and goals.

Sampling techniques fall into two broad families. In **probability sampling**, every member of the population has a known chance of being selected, which lets you generalise statistically to the population. In **non-probability sampling**, selection isn’t random; it is often more practical, and it is the norm in qualitative research, but it limits statistical generalisation.

## What are the probability sampling techniques?

### Simple random sampling

Every member of the population has an equal chance of being selected, for example by drawing names or using a random number generator on a complete list.

It is simple in principle and works well when you have a complete list of a population that is easy to reach. It can be expensive or impractical when the population is large or spread out.

### Stratified sampling

You divide the population into subgroups, or strata, based on a characteristic that matters for your study, such as age group, sex or region. Then you draw a random sample from each stratum. Often the number drawn from each stratum is in proportion to its share of the population; sometimes small groups are deliberately oversampled so there are enough of them to analyse.

Stratified sampling makes sure every important subgroup is represented, which is valuable when the population is diverse.

### Cluster sampling

You divide the population into naturally occurring groups, or clusters, such as schools, villages or hospitals. Then you randomly select some clusters, and include everyone in them, or a random sample of people within them.

Cluster sampling is economical when a population is spread over a wide area or hard to list as individuals. But people within a cluster tend to be alike, so a cluster sample usually gives less precise results than a simple random sample of the same size. You need a larger sample to make up for it, and your analysis must take the clustering into account.

### Systematic sampling

You choose a random starting point on a list, then select every *n*th member after it, for example every tenth name.

It is easier to carry out than simple random sampling, and usually gives similar results. The risk is a hidden pattern in the list: if the list repeats in a cycle that matches your interval, your sample can be badly skewed.

## What are the non-probability sampling techniques?

### Snowball sampling

You start with a small group of participants, who then refer you to others in their networks, who refer others in turn.

Snowball sampling is useful when the population is hidden or hard to reach, such as marginalised or stigmatised communities whose members may be reluctant to respond to conventional recruitment. Because participants recruit people like themselves, the sample may not represent the wider population, so be careful about how far you generalise.

### Other common techniques

Three other non-probability techniques are widely used:

- **Purposive sampling:** deliberately choosing participants who can best inform your research question, because of their role or experience. It is the most common approach in qualitative research.
- **Convenience sampling:** recruiting whoever is easiest to reach, such as students in your own department. It is quick and cheap, but the most open to bias.
- **Quota sampling:** recruiting until you have set numbers of people with particular characteristics, without random selection.

## How to choose a sampling technique

### Know your population

Is the population fairly uniform, or does it contain distinct subgroups that matter for your question? If subgroups matter, stratified sampling, or a purposive sample that deliberately includes them, will serve you better than a simple random sample.

### Be clear about your objectives

Do you want to generalise to the whole population, or understand one group in depth? If you need to estimate how common something is in a population, you need a probability sample. If you want rich understanding of a particular experience, a purposive sample is usually right.

### Be realistic about resources

Consider the time, money and help you have. Some techniques, such as simple random sampling of a scattered population, can cost far more than others. Choose a method that balances practicality with the accuracy your question needs.

### Watch for sampling bias

Think about where bias could enter your sample. **Selection bias** arises when some members of the population are more likely to be chosen than others. **Volunteer bias** arises because the people who agree to take part may differ from those who don’t. **Non-response bias** arises when the people who don’t respond differ from those who do. Choose a technique, and a recruitment process, that keeps these as small as you can, and report them honestly.

### Match sampling to your data collection

Some ways of collecting data go more naturally with some sampling techniques. A large online survey suits probability or quota sampling; in-depth interviews usually suit purposive or snowball sampling. Make sure your sampling and data collection work together.

### Decide how many

Sample size matters as much as technique. In quantitative research, a power analysis or sample size calculation tells you how many participants you need. In qualitative research, you decide how much data is enough for your question and approach, and explain how you decided.

Sampling for surveys is covered further in [surveys and questionnaires](/blog/surveys-and-questionnaires), and for interviews in [research interviews](/blog/research-interviews).

## In short

Choosing a sampling technique isn’t just a practical detail. It determines how far your findings can be trusted and how far they can be applied. Know your population, be clear about whether you need to generalise or to understand in depth, be realistic about resources, and be honest about the biases your method may introduce. Then explain your choice clearly in your methodology.
