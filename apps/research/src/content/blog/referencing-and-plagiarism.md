In 2025 I shared thirty lessons from my years as a researcher, one at a time. This piece brings together two of them about the same subject: giving other people’s work the credit it deserves.

## Lesson 14: Referencing is about credibility, not just compliance

Referencing isn’t just an academic hoop to jump through. It is a mark of credibility.

It shows that your work is built on solid foundations and that you respect other people’s intellectual work. It isn’t only about following a style guide. It is about showing that your work can be trusted.

Accurate referencing:

- **strengthens your arguments.** You are saying, “I’ve done my homework.”
- **guides readers to your sources.** You are saying, “Here is where you can check my claims.”
- **protects you from accusations of plagiarism.** You are saying, “This is what I built on, and this is what is mine.”

Think of your references as the scaffolding that holds your research together. Some readers never look at it, but the structure depends on it.

> **Pro tip:** check every reference against the source itself, not against someone else’s reference list. Errors get copied from paper to paper, and a citation to a source that doesn’t say what you claim it says does more damage than no citation at all. This matters even more if you use AI tools to help with searching: one study found that a large share of the references chatbots produced for short literature reviews were for papers that don’t exist ([Walters and Wilder, 2023](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41598-023-41032-5)).

For the formats of APA, MLA, Chicago and Harvard, see the references section of [structuring a research paper, part 2](/blog/structuring-a-research-paper-results-discussion-conclusion-references).

## Lesson 15: Plagiarism is more than copy and paste

Many people think plagiarism only happens when you copy text word for word.

In reality, it also includes:

- **paraphrasing poorly without citing the source**, so the wording changes but the ideas, and often the structure, are still someone else’s;
- **presenting someone else’s ideas as your own**, even in completely new words;
- **failing to acknowledge indirect influences**, such as a framework, an argument or a line of reasoning you took from another writer.

Plagiarism isn’t just an academic offence. It is a breach of trust.

The good news is that you can avoid it, through careful citation, good note-taking and the habit of attributing ideas generously.

> **Pro tip:** most accidental plagiarism starts in your notes. When you copy a passage, put it in quotation marks and write down the source and page number straight away. When you summarise, write in your own words with the source closed, then go back and check you haven’t kept the original’s phrasing. If you can’t tell later whether a line in your notes is yours or the author’s, you have a problem.

Protect your integrity. Your credibility is worth more than any shortcut.

## What ties these together

Referencing and avoiding plagiarism are two sides of the same habit: being honest about where your ideas came from. Cite carefully, keep clean notes, check your references against the sources, and give credit generously. Readers will trust your work more because they can see exactly what it stands on.
