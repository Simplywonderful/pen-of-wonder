Imagine setting out to explore unfamiliar country. You have a strong sense of purpose and a map. In research, the map is your research design: the plan that guides you from your question to your answer.

This guide covers what a research design is, the main types, and how to choose one that fits your study. The examples are illustrations.

## What is a research design?

A research design is the overall plan for your study: its strategy, its methods and how it is organised. It sets out how you will collect, analyse and interpret your data, and it shapes every decision that follows.

Choosing a design is like choosing the right tool for a job. A good tool used for the wrong job still produces poor work.

## What are the main types of research design?

### Experimental

In an experimental design, you deliberately change one or more **independent variables** and measure the effect on one or more **dependent variables**, under controlled conditions. With random assignment of participants to conditions, experiments allow you to draw conclusions about cause and effect.

Experiments don’t have to happen in a laboratory. **Field experiments** take place in real-world settings, such as schools, clinics or workplaces, which makes their findings more true to everyday life, at the cost of some control.

**Example:** randomly assigning classes to a new reading programme or to their usual teaching, then comparing reading scores at the end of the term.

### Correlational

A correlational design looks for relationships between variables as they occur naturally, without manipulating anything. Statistics such as Pearson’s correlation coefficient measure how strongly two variables are related.

Correlational research is valuable for finding patterns, especially where an experiment would be impossible or unethical. But a correlation alone doesn’t show that one variable causes the other.

**Example:** measuring whether students who sleep more tend to have higher grades.

### Descriptive

A descriptive design sets out to describe the characteristics or behaviour of a population or phenomenon as it is, using surveys, interviews or observation. It gives an overview of the current situation and often lays the groundwork for further research and new hypotheses.

**Example:** a survey of how many households in a district have access to clean water, and how far they travel to collect it.

### Qualitative

A qualitative design explores complex phenomena in depth, through methods such as interviews, focus groups and participant observation. It focuses on people’s own experiences and perspectives, and on the meanings and contexts that numbers can miss.

**Example:** interviews with nurses about how they make decisions during night shifts.

### Mixed methods

A mixed methods design combines quantitative and qualitative approaches in one study, collecting and analysing both numerical and textual data. It can give a fuller understanding of a complex problem, and lets you compare findings from different kinds of evidence.

**Example:** a survey to measure how widely a new policy is known, followed by interviews to understand why some groups haven’t heard of it.

Each design has its own strengths and limitations. None is best in general. The question is which is best for your study.

## How to choose a research design

### Match the design to your question and hypotheses

Your research question, and your hypotheses if you have them, point you towards the design. A question about cause calls for an experiment where one is possible. A question about how things are related calls for a correlational design. A question about what people experience calls for a qualitative one. Make sure the design and the question work together.

### Take the context into account

Think about the historical, social and cultural context of your question. A design that works well in one setting may not in another. Choose one that respects your setting and lets you capture what matters in it.

### Think about how you will collect data

Different designs call for different ways of collecting data: surveys, interviews, observation or analysis of records and archives. Choose a design whose data collection methods can give you reliable answers to your question, and that you have the skills to carry out.

### Balance control and realism

Designs range from tightly controlled laboratory experiments to observation of everyday life. More control makes it easier to show cause and effect; more realism makes the findings more likely to apply in the real world, which researchers call ecological validity. Decide which matters more for your question, and choose a design that strikes the right balance.

### Be realistic about time, money and access

Research never happens in a vacuum. Consider how much time you have, your budget, and whether you can actually reach the participants or data you need. A design that balances ambition with what is practical is one you can finish.

### Pilot it

A small pilot study lets you test whether your design is feasible and works as intended. It shows up problems while they are still easy to fix, before you commit to the full study.

Your design follows from your question, so start with [research questions and hypotheses](/blog/research-questions-and-hypotheses). For experiments in more detail, see [experimental research](/blog/experimental-research).

## In short

Your research design is the plan your whole study rests on. Know the main types, experimental, correlational, descriptive, qualitative and mixed methods, and what each can and can’t tell you. Then choose the one that fits your question, your setting and your resources, and test it with a pilot before you begin. The right design makes everything that follows easier to defend.
