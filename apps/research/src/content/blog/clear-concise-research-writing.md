Research writing links the work of finding things out with the work of telling people what you found. Most papers follow a familiar structure: question, method, results, conclusions. But structure alone doesn’t make a paper readable. Clear and concise writing is what makes sure your message is understood.

Many academics make the same mistake: they over-complicate their writing, lean on jargon and produce dense papers that are hard to get through. The challenge is to balance complexity with simplicity and thoroughness with clarity. This guide covers how to do that, the mistakes to avoid, and some techniques that go a little beyond the usual tips.

## What research writing is for

Research writing covers journal articles, conference papers, theses, dissertations and reports. Whatever the form, the aim is the same: to add something to a field by sharing new knowledge or a new perspective, in a way other people can check and build on. If readers can’t follow your writing, they can’t do either.

## Part 1: Habits of clear research writing

### Know your audience and adjust your language

Clear writing starts with knowing who is reading. Experts in your own field? Academics in general? Readers from other disciplines? Policymakers?

You can use specialist terms when writing for specialists, but simplify for a wider readership. For a general academic audience, “ways of understanding knowledge” may serve you better than “epistemological paradigms”.

### Open with a clear statement of what the paper argues

Your thesis statement, or statement of purpose, should tell readers exactly what the paper does. It gives them a path to follow.

Instead of: “This paper explores the various impacts of climate change on agriculture.”

Try: “This paper argues that climate change reduces crop yields by altering rainfall patterns and raising temperatures.”

The second version tells the reader what you will show, which makes everything after it easier to follow.

### Use simple, precise words

Technical terms are sometimes necessary, but too much jargon pushes readers away. Say things in the plainest words that keep the meaning exact.

Instead of: “Utilise the apparatus to ascertain the mass.”

Try: “Use the balance to measure the mass.”

Notice that simpler doesn’t mean looser. “Mass” stays “mass”: changing it to “weight” would make the sentence simpler and wrong.

### Follow a clear outline

A well-structured paper leads readers through your argument. Use headings and subheadings to break the work into sections they can navigate, such as Introduction, Methods, Results, Discussion and Conclusion, and make each section lead naturally into the next.

### Be concise, and edit hard

Concise writing cuts every word that isn’t working. When you edit, remove redundant words, phrases and whole sentences.

Instead of: “Due to the fact that the experiment was conducted over a period of time that was relatively short…”

Try: “Because the experiment ran for a short time…”

“Due to the fact that” is one of the most common candidates for cutting. So are “in order to” (use “to”), “it is important to note that” (usually delete), and “a total of” before a number.

### Prefer the active voice

The active voice makes clear who did what, and it usually makes sentences shorter.

Instead of: “The experiment was conducted by the researchers.”

Try: “The researchers conducted the experiment.”

The passive still has its place in methods sections, where the action matters more than who did it. Check your target journal’s style; many now prefer the active voice throughout.

### Define terms, and give examples

When you introduce a new or difficult idea, define it and then show it.

For example, if you discuss “resilience in ecosystems”, give a definition and then a concrete case, such as the ability of some plants to survive drought.

### Use visuals where they help

Tables, charts and graphs can make complex information easier to grasp. Label them properly and make sure each one earns its place.

For example, a graph showing how agricultural yields fall as temperatures rise, with a clear legend and a descriptive caption, can say in one glance what would take a paragraph to explain.

## Part 2: Mistakes that make papers hard work

### Not planning before writing

Starting to write without a plan is one of the worst mistakes. Skipping the outline often produces a disorganised, repetitive paper. Before you start, write down your main arguments and the evidence that supports each one. A good outline works as a map, so the paper moves logically from one section to the next.

### Not defining important terms

Don’t assume readers know every technical term. Define key concepts precisely, and explain how they relate to your study. If your paper is about “neural plasticity”, say what you mean by it before you build on it.

### Breaking the flow of the argument

Jumping from point to point without transitions makes readers work out the connections themselves. Use linking sentences to show how each section follows from the last. For example: “Building on the previous discussion of neural plasticity, this section examines its implications for cognitive rehabilitation.”

### Relying too heavily on quotations

Citing sources matters, but long quotations can drown out your own analysis. Summarise ideas in your own words, with a citation, and offer your interpretation. Save direct quotations for wording that genuinely matters.

### Ignoring counterarguments

A paper that doesn’t acknowledge other views looks weaker, not stronger. If your study supports one theory, discuss the competing explanations and explain why your evidence favours yours. It shows you have thought it through, and it answers the questions a reviewer would otherwise ask.

### Inconsistent citations and formatting

Mixed citation styles and inconsistent formatting confuse readers and make the work look careless. Choose one style (APA, Harvard, Vancouver or whatever your journal requires) and apply it to every reference. A reference manager such as Zotero or Mendeley makes this much easier.

### Skipping the revision

Rushing to submit without a thorough review leaves errors and weak claims in place. Plan for several rounds of editing: one for structure and argument, one for clarity, one for grammar and formatting. Ask a supervisor, colleague or mentor to read it too.

## Part 3: Techniques beyond the usual tips

### Open with an accurate hook

An introduction should make the reader want to continue. A surprising finding, a sharp question or a short case can do that. But check any statistic you open with.

A popular hook claims that most scientific papers are never cited. It doesn’t hold up. The claim traces back to a 1990 report that counted letters, corrections and editorials along with research papers. When Nature analysed 39 million research papers and reviews in the Web of Science, about 21% had not been cited, and for biomedical papers published in 2006 the figure was 4% (Van Noorden, 2017). Opening your paper with a statistic like the first one, in front of reviewers who know the field, does more harm than opening with none.

### Start each paragraph with a strong topic sentence

The first sentence of each paragraph should state its main idea. Readers who skim, which includes many reviewers, can then follow your argument from topic sentences alone.

For example: “Neural plasticity, the brain’s ability to reorganise itself, plays a central role in cognitive recovery after stroke.”

### Use the CARS model for your introduction

Swales’ (1990) Create a Research Space (CARS) model describes how strong research article introductions work, in three moves:

1. **Establish a territory:** show that the topic matters and summarise what is known.
2. **Establish a niche:** point to a gap, a contradiction or an unanswered question in that work.
3. **Occupy the niche:** say how your study addresses that gap, and what it found.

For example: summarise the current state of research on brain plasticity; note where the evidence is thin or contradictory; then explain how your study fills that gap. The model is about introductions, but it works just as well for the short literature review that sits inside most journal introductions.

### Justify your methods, don’t just describe them

Explain why you chose your methods as well as what they were. Readers, and reviewers, accept a method more readily when they can see the reasoning.

For example: “Because the question concerned activity across brain networks, we used fMRI, which offers high spatial resolution and allows detailed mapping of brain function.”

### Anticipate criticism

Address likely criticisms before a reviewer raises them. It shows thoroughness and saves a round of revision.

For example: “Because our sample came from one demographic group, future research should include more diverse populations to test whether these findings generalise.”

## In short

Clear, concise research writing takes practice and attention to detail. Know your readers, say what you argue, choose plain and precise words, structure the paper so it leads somewhere, and edit until every sentence earns its place. The goal isn’t to sound academic. It’s to be understood.
