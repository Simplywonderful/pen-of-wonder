## What did this first campaign look like?

Someone posted on their status that they needed an ad expert, and a friend sent it to me. I reached out. It turned out they didn't need someone to run the ads. They needed someone to talk through what they were seeing.

They're a social media manager and content creator at a startup, and they'd been handed the ads for a product. They're new to Meta ads and had been learning from articles and videos. That helps, but it isn't the same as running a campaign. They also seemed to want to figure it out themselves, so I gave direction and didn't take over.

The goal was sign-ups to a platform. Here is what I found.

## Does your campaign objective match your goal?

The campaign objective was Engagement. They were surprised nobody was messaging them, and as far as I could tell, sign-ups weren't being tracked.

A campaign set to engagement is built to get engagement, not sign-ups. If the result you want is a sign-up, the campaign has to be set up for that, as I explain in [why the wrong campaign objective wastes the learning, not just the budget](/blog/wrong-campaign-objective-awareness-vs-engagement) and in [how a sales campaign differs from a leads campaign](/blog/sales-campaigns-vs-leads-campaigns-meta-pixel-tracking).

## Can you count the result you want?

You can't judge a sign-up campaign you can't count. Before launching, make sure the sign-up is tracked as a result, so Meta can optimise toward it and you can read what each one costs.

## Is one creative enough?

They were running one creative, a video. They believed it was so good it would do the job. It might be. But you can't call a creative a winner before it runs.

I suggested at least five creatives, UGC videos if possible, since the first one was UGC, or other variations. Then the audience tells you which one works, through the numbers after several days, and you scale on results, not on preference. The reasoning is in [why I recommend at least twenty ad creatives, not three](/blog/why-you-need-twenty-ad-creatives-not-three), and [the hook you like best is often not the winner](/blog/the-hook-you-think-will-work-probably-wont).

## Which numbers show whether an ad is working?

After two days, they were worried about impressions and reach. Impressions and reach aren't what tells you whether an ad is working. For a sign-up campaign, it's what each sign-up costs. And two days is too early to judge anything. I explain the window I use in [how long to let a Meta ad run before judging it](/blog/how-long-to-let-a-meta-ad-run-before-judging-it).

## Why isn't my daily budget being spent?

The ₦3,000 daily budget wasn't being fully spent. I can't say for certain why. The audience was limited to a few cities, and I didn't ask how big it was. The campaign was also set to engagement. Either could play a part.

I'm naming both as possibilities, not as a diagnosis, because I didn't have enough information to be sure.

## Summary: what I found and what to do

| What I found | What to do |
| --- | --- |
| Goal was sign-ups, objective was Engagement | Set the objective for the result you want |
| Sign-ups didn't appear to be tracked | Track the sign-up so you can count and judge it |
| One creative | Run at least five, UGC videos if possible, then scale on results |
| Watching impressions and reach | Watch what each sign-up costs |
| Judging after two days | Give it several days |
| Budget not fully spent | Check audience size and objective, I couldn't confirm the cause |

## Before you open Ads Manager

If you're running ads for the first time, decide the one result you want before you open Ads Manager, set up the campaign so you can track it, and give it several days. If you're unsure who you're targeting, start with [the one question to answer before you open Ads Manager](/blog/the-one-question-before-you-open-ads-manager). And if you want to know whether you have enough budget to get a fair test, read [how much ad budget you actually need](/blog/how-much-ad-budget-do-i-actually-need).
