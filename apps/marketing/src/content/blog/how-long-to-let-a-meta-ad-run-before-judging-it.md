## How long should you let a Meta ad run before judging it?

My own rule is 10 to 15 days. That gives the algorithm time to test your ads and work out what is landing, so you're reading a pattern instead of noise. Deciding on day two means deciding on noise.

> **10 to 15 days**: How long I let new Meta ads run before judging them. A working rule from my own experience, not an official Meta threshold.

Meta describes the early period of a new ad set as the learning phase, when its system is still working out how to deliver your ads. Its Help Centre explains how it works in [About the learning phase for ad sets](https://www.facebook.com/business/help/112167992830700). Check that page for the current thresholds, because platform numbers change and I don't want to quote one that has moved.

If you're still deciding whether you have enough budget to get through that window, read [how much ad budget you actually need](/blog/how-much-ad-budget-do-i-actually-need) first.

## What should you look at when you judge a creative?

Two things for each creative: how much it spent, and what it got for that money. Depending on the campaign, the result is a click, a message, a lead or a sale.

| What you see | What it means | What I do |
| --- | --- | --- |
| High spend, little result | The creative is spending money without earning it | Pause it |
| Good result at a good cost | The creative is doing its job | Keep it and scale it |

## How do you decide which ads to pause?

Pause the weakest: the ones that spent the most and produced the least. Judge each creative against the others in the same campaign, not against a number you picked in advance.

## How do you scale a winning ad creative?

Scaling is more than turning up the budget. I do two things.

1. **Test the winner with new copy.** Duplicate the ad set, run the winning creative with different copy, and compare it against the original.
2. **Vary the creative, keep the copy.** Make variations of the winning creative by changing the hook, the offer or the call to action while keeping the same copy as before.

Changing one thing at a time means that whatever moves in the results comes from the thing you changed. Change three things at once and you won't know which one did it, which is exactly the lesson from [a rebuilt campaign where I changed three things together](/blog/i-rebuilt-a-lead-campaign-what-it-does-and-doesnt-prove).

## How many creatives should you run?

The more the better. For my own ads I'd run at least 20. That's a lot for a client, so for clients I advise at least five, images and videos, and I lean towards video. The case for going big is in [why I recommend at least twenty ad creatives, not three](/blog/why-you-need-twenty-ad-creatives-not-three), and [the hook is the part most worth testing](/blog/the-hook-you-think-will-work-probably-wont).

## What if the campaign still underperforms after the test window?

If the numbers are still weak after the window and your copy, creative and targeting all look right, the problem is probably elsewhere. I walk through where I look in [when copy, creative and targeting are all right and the campaign still underperforms](/blog/diagnosing-a-campaign-that-should-be-working). And if you're tempted to change things because a client or a gut feeling is pushing you, [that pressure is how working campaigns get reset](/blog/when-a-working-campaign-feels-like-a-failure).

None of this is complicated. It takes patience before the decision and discipline after it.
