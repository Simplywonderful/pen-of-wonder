## The habit research teaches that marketing rarely does

My background isn't marketing. It's research: the kind where you design a study, control your variables, measure your outcomes, and defend your conclusions with data. That discipline does something specific to how you think.

You stop accepting "I think this ad will work" as good enough. Instead you start asking a different set of questions before you spend a single naira:

- What does the data actually say, not what do we hope it says?
- What's the click-through rate telling us about the hook?
- Why did this audience respond, and that one didn't?
- What needs to change in the next iteration, based on evidence rather than opinion?

That's not the typical approach to social media advertising. Most of the industry runs on pattern-matching and confidence. But it's exactly how good advertising should be approached, because an ad account is, structurally, an experiment. You're testing a hypothesis about who will respond to what message, and the platform hands you the data to confirm or kill that hypothesis within days.

## Applying it commercially

Over a period of months I applied these skills commercially: running paid social campaigns on Meta, LinkedIn, TikTok, and X. It wasn't a leap into something unfamiliar. It was a natural extension of how I already think: define the question, design the test, read the result, adjust.

One of the earlier tests of that approach was a 15-day Meta ads campaign for a professional services brand: a résumé-writing offer, positioned as "CV delivered in 7 hours or your money back."

It brought in messaging conversations from cold traffic, and some of them became paying clients straight away, with others still warming up.

Not a viral result. A real one: built on reading the data, refining the creative based on what the numbers said rather than what looked good, and letting the platform's learning phase actually run its course. That's the same standard I bring to [every campaign I work on](/services): structured strategy, honest tracking, and decisions that follow evidence instead of preference.
