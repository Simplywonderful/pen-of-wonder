## It might look like I'm shooting myself in the foot

Something I'm realizing as I go: you should not pay for ads until you've sold the thing without them.

I work in paid advertising, so I know how that sounds. But I'm here to help as honestly as I can, not to take money from people whose offer isn't ready for it.

## The trap: excitement makes an idea feel proven

Here's the trap. You've heard a real problem described in conversations with friends, family, even strangers. You listened, you spotted it, and you built a solution. That deserves credit. But then the excitement does something quiet: it makes the idea feel proven before a single person has paid for it.

## The rule: before ads, sell it by hand

So here's the rule I'd hold to. Before ads, sell it by hand. Go directly to the people who told you about the problem, show them the solution, and ask for the sale. My own benchmark is 5 to 10 paying customers from organic effort alone. It's a rule of thumb, not a law, but I'd want some version of it before putting money into a campaign.

> **5 to 10**: Paying customers from organic effort alone, my rule-of-thumb benchmark before putting money into a campaign

## Even better: make the pitch before you finish building

At ECIC, Raphael Ajayi talked about pitching your solution to at least five people who have the problem, to see if they understand it well enough to buy. That landed with me because it's the same idea from the other side. If the people who described the problem won't pay for the fix, you don't have a business yet. Either your message needs refining until they truly understand what it solves, or this solution isn't the right one right now. It might sell later. Not today.

One catch worth adding: a friend saying "I'd buy that" is not the same as a friend paying for it. The test is money moving, not encouragement. If nobody will pay you even once, that's information, and it's much cheaper to learn it before an ad budget is involved.

## Why smart people skip this

If it's so obvious, why do smart people skip it? Because we decide emotionally and justify with logic. That line usually describes how customers buy, but it describes how founders spend too. When you're excited, ad spend feels like the logical next step. Often it's just the excitement looking for permission.

Ads amplify what already works. They can't create demand the offer hasn't earned, and they get expensive quickly when asked to. It's the same principle behind [the three questions I ask before running anyone's ads](/blog/three-questions-before-i-run-your-ads).
